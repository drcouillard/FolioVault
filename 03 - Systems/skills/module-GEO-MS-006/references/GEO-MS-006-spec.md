## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:** Direction is not intuitive — it is encoded. Cardinal and intermediate directions are a shared notation system that makes spatial communication precise and unambiguous. A compass rose is not a decoration on a map; it is the tool that anchors all directional reasoning to an agreed reference point. Without it, "turn left" depends on where you're standing. With it, "travel northeast" means the same thing to everyone holding that map.

**Acceptable Evidence:** A student can: (1) identify all four cardinal and four intermediate directions on a compass rose without prompting; (2) use directional notation to describe the relationship between two locations on a map; (3) select the correct direction of travel for a described geographic movement; (4) explain why cardinal directions are fixed references while relative directions are observer-dependent.

## Section 3 — Wonder Hook

**Hook Type:** Contradiction **ARCS Condition:** Attention

> You've given someone directions hundreds of times. "Turn left at the light. Go straight for two blocks. It's on the right." Those directions work — until the person is coming from a different starting point. Then "left" becomes "right," and your directions fall apart.
> 
> Cardinal directions don't do that. North is north regardless of which way you're facing. That's not a small distinction — it's the difference between directions that work for one person and directions that work for anyone.

**Relevance hook (ARCS: Relevance):** Every map in this course has a north arrow or compass rose. Every navigation system — GPS, nautical charts, aeronautical maps, hiking trails — uses the same cardinal system. This module explains why that system exists, how to read it, and how to use it to describe geographic relationships precisely.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

### The Problem with Relative Direction

Relative directions (left, right, forward, back) are observer-dependent. They change when the observer turns. Cardinal directions solve this by anchoring direction to Earth's geographic poles — references that don't move and don't depend on the observer.

### The Four Cardinal Directions

|Direction|Reference|Abbreviation|
|---|---|---|
|North|Toward the North Pole|N|
|South|Toward the South Pole|S|
|East|Direction of Earth's rotation|E|
|West|Opposite Earth's rotation|W|

[VERIFY: confirm "direction of Earth's rotation" is standard and accurate for 9th grade; confirm geographic vs. magnetic north distinction below]

### The Four Intermediate Directions

|Direction|Between|Abbreviation|
|---|---|---|
|Northeast|North and East|NE|
|Southeast|South and East|SE|
|Southwest|South and West|SW|
|Northwest|North and West|NW|

**Naming convention:** N-S component always named first. "Northeast" not "eastnorth." Standard in English-language cartography — not optional. [VERIFY: confirm N-S first convention is universal]

### The Compass Rose

The compass rose displays cardinal and intermediate directions — orienting the reader to the map's coordinate system. Not decorative. Modern maps often use a north arrow (simpler) rather than a full compass rose. If neither appears, north-up convention is assumed — a convention, not a rule. [VERIFY: confirm north-up as convention not rule; confirm north arrow vs. compass rose usage]

### Geographic North vs. Magnetic North

- **Geographic north (true north):** Toward Earth's geographic North Pole. Fixed. This is what cardinal directions reference.
- **Magnetic north:** Toward the magnetic North Pole — shifts over time. A compass needle points here, not geographic north.
- **Magnetic declination:** The difference between the two at any given location.

For standard map reading, geographic north is the reference. Magnetic declination matters for field navigation — noted here as a scope flag, not developed further. [VERIFY: confirm appropriate depth for 9th grade]

### Going Deeper — Navigation & Surveying

The full directional measurement system is called azimuth or bearing — expressed in degrees clockwise from north (0° = north, 090° = east, 180° = south, 270° = west). Used in aeronautical navigation, military mapping, and surveying. Studied in navigation courses and trigonometry.

### Using Directions to Describe Geographic Relationships

Cardinal directions describe spatial relationship between two locations — not a path of travel. "Cairo is northeast of Nairobi" = position. "To travel from Nairobi to Cairo, travel generally northward" = movement. Both valid; both require a reference point and map orientation.

## Section 5 — Big Idea Callback (Passive)

> What's the big idea here? Left and right only work if everyone is facing the same way. Cardinal directions work for everyone, always — because they're anchored to Earth itself, not to the person giving the directions. That's what makes them useful on maps: the map doesn't know which way you're facing, but it knows which way north is. Once you do too, you can orient yourself to anything on it.

## Section 6 — Worked Example

**ARCS: Confidence · Gagné Event 5 · Procedural**

Scenario: Map of East Africa with north arrow. Student determines direction of travel from Nairobi, Kenya to Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

**Step 1 — Confirm orientation.** North arrow = north is up. South is down, east is right, west is left.

**Step 2 — Locate both cities.** Nairobi: southern Kenya, map center. Addis Ababa: Ethiopia, above and slightly left of Nairobi.

**Step 3 — Describe the relationship.** Addis Ababa is above (north) and slightly left (west) of Nairobi = northwest.

**Step 4 — State direction of travel.** Travel northwest from Nairobi to reach Addis Ababa. [VERIFY: confirm Addis Ababa is northwest of Nairobi]

**Key demonstration:** Directional reasoning requires two anchors — map orientation (north arrow) and relative position of locations. Both must be established first.

## Section 7 — Gagné Event 2 ("Before the Questions")

- Cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) are fixed references anchored to Earth's geographic poles — they do not change with the observer's orientation
- Intermediate directions (NE, SE, SW, NW) fall exactly between the cardinal directions — always named with the north-south component first
- A compass rose or north arrow anchors the map's orientation — without it, directional reasoning has no fixed reference
- Directional descriptions state the relationship between two locations — they require a reference point and a map orientation to be meaningful

### Q1 [NEW] [Bloom: Remember] [Difficulty: Foundational]

Eight-point compass rose. Which direction is exactly between South and West?

- A) Southeast
- B) Northwest
- C) Southwest ✓
- D) West-southwest

**Misconception (C correct):** SW sits between S and W. B is the opposite quadrant. D introduces a secondary intercardinal not in the eight-point system.

### Q2 [NEW] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

Seoul is above and to the left of Tokyo on a north-up map. Which intermediate direction, and what naming convention confirms it?

- A) Eastnorth — east before north alphabetically
- B) Northwest — N-S component named first ✓
- C) Westnorth — west-first on Asian maps
- D) Southwest — left on a north-up map is always south

**Misconception (B correct):** NW is correct; N-S first is the universal convention. A inverts it. C invents a regional exception. D confuses left (relative) with west (cardinal) and adds a directional error. [VERIFY: confirm Seoul is northwest of Tokyo]

### Q3 [NEW] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

Two hikers standing back-to-back at the same location: Hiker A says "the river is to my left," Hiker B says "the river is to my right." Both true. Asked to use cardinal directions instead — what happens?

- A) Their directions still disagree — cardinal directions also observer-dependent
- B) Their cardinal directions agree — both give the same cardinal direction for the river ✓
- C) Cardinal directions can't describe nearby locations
- D) Only the hiker facing north is correct

**Misconception (B correct):** Cardinal directions are observer-independent — both hikers state the same direction. A fails the cardinal/relative distinction. D applies relative direction logic to cardinal directions.

### Q4 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-003 — Reading coordinates] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

City A: 34°S, 18°E (Cape Town). City B: 33°N, 44°E (Baghdad). Direction of travel A → B?

- A) Southeast
- B) Southwest
- C) Northwest
- D) North and slightly east ✓

**Misconception (D correct):** 34°S → 33°N = moving north (crossing equator). 18°E → 44°E = moving east. Dominant movement is north; eastward shift secondary. C and A misread latitude direction. B reverses both. [VERIFY: confirm coordinates; confirm directional relationship]

### Q6 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-005 — Map type fitness for purpose] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

Search-and-rescue team tells incoming helicopter: "survivors are 2km northwest of the burned-out building." Helicopter crew approaching from east, doesn't know team leader's facing direction. Will this communication work?

- A) No — "northwest" is relative to the team leader's facing direction
- B) Yes — cardinal directions are fixed references; northwest means the same regardless of observer ✓
- C) Only if the helicopter crew also has a compass
- D) No — should have used GPS coordinates

**Misconception (B correct):** Cardinal directions are observer-independent — the core module concept. A imports relative direction logic. C adds unnecessary condition. D isn't wrong about GPS but doesn't make cardinal direction communication incorrect.

### Q7 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-004 — Projection selection for purpose] [Bloom: Analyze] [Difficulty: Challenge]

Azimuthal Equidistant projection centered on Cairo. North is not consistently "up." Student wants to determine what direction Cairo lies from Sydney. What challenge does this projection create?

- A) No challenge — cardinal directions work the same on all projections
- B) Distance is distorted, making direction estimate inaccurate
- C) On a projection centered on Cairo, straight lines from Cairo represent true direction FROM Cairo — but direction TO Cairo from Sydney requires knowing where north is at Sydney's position on this projection, which is not immediately obvious ✓
- D) Azimuthal projections cannot show direction at all

**Misconception (C correct):** Azimuthal Equidistant preserves direction and distance FROM center only. Reading direction to the center from a peripheral location requires reasoning about north at that location. A hasn't applied GEO-MS-004 concept. B confuses distance distortion with directional distortion — AE doesn't distort distance from center. D inverts the actual property. [VERIFY: confirm AE preserves direction and distance from center; confirm challenge described is accurate]

### Q8 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-002 — Fitness for purpose] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

Park ranger: Sign A = "Waterfall — 1.2km to the left." Sign B = "Waterfall — 1.2km northwest." Which is more useful to hikers approaching from different directions?

- A) Sign A — "left" is more natural
- B) Sign B — "northwest" works regardless of approach direction; "left" only works for one approach ✓
- C) Both equally useful
- D) Neither — use GPS coordinates

**Misconception (B correct):** Relative directions fail when observer orientation is unknown. Sign B gives the same information to everyone. A prioritizes familiarity over function. C underestimates observer orientation's effect on relative direction. D misses the point that cardinal direction is precisely the right tool here.

## Section 10 — Big Idea Callback (Active — EIB)

> What's the big idea here? A shipping company needs to route a cargo vessel from Cape Town, South Africa to Mumbai, India. The navigator has a standard north-up world map and a compass rose.
> 
> Walk through it:
> 
> 1. Describe the general direction of travel from Cape Town to Mumbai — use cardinal or intermediate directions and explain what map evidence supports your answer.
> 2. The navigator notices the map uses a Mercator projection. Does the projection affect the directional reading in this case — and how would you advise the navigator?
> 3. If the ship's compass shows magnetic north is 8° east of the map's north arrow, does that affect the navigator's directional plan — and what should they do about it?
> 
> There's no single right answer. The reasoning is the evidence.

**EIB Rubric (7 points — mastery at 5/7):**

- States northeast (or north and east) as direction of travel; cites latitude and longitude movement as evidence (2 pts)
- Addresses Mercator — notes that Mercator preserves direction (rhumb lines) so directional reading is valid for this purpose; OR notes that great circle routes differ from rhumb lines on Mercator (2 pts — either reasoning accepted)
- Addresses magnetic declination — identifies it as relevant for compass use but not for map reading; recommends the navigator apply the 8° correction when using the compass against the map (2 pts)
- Reasoning is coherent and evidence-based throughout (1 pt)
