## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:** A map without a legend is a picture, not a tool. Legends are not decoration — they are the contract between the mapmaker and the reader, encoding decisions about what counts as worth showing and how to show it. Map types make that contract explicit at a larger scale: thematic maps, reference maps, and navigation maps are not interchangeable; each encodes different priorities and serves different purposes. Reading a legend fluently means reading the mapmaker's argument.

**Acceptable Evidence:** A student can: (1) extract geographic information from an unfamiliar map using its legend without assistance; (2) identify the map type from its design and purpose — not just its label; (3) explain why a specific map type is or is not appropriate for a given task; (4) recognize when a map's legend is misleading or incomplete and describe what is missing.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

### What a Legend Does

A legend — sometimes called a key — translates the visual language of a map into meaning. Every symbol, color, line weight, and pattern on a map is a decision the cartographer made. The legend records those decisions so the reader can decode them.

Legends are not standardized. A blue line on one map means a river. On another, it means a highway. On a third, it means a political boundary. The legend is the only authoritative source for what any symbol means on that specific map. Reading a legend before reading a map is not optional — it is the first act of map literacy.

### What Legends Encode

|Type|What it shows|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Point symbols|Discrete locations — cities, airports, peaks|● = capital city; △ = mountain summit|
|Line symbols|Linear features — roads, rivers, borders|Solid line = paved road; dashed = unpaved|
|Area symbols|Zones, regions, land cover|Green fill = forest; yellow = desert|
|Graduated symbols|Quantity at a location — scaled to data|Larger circle = larger city population|

[VERIFY: confirm symbol type taxonomy is consistent with standard cartographic terminology]

### Map Types — The Three Families

|Map Type|Primary purpose|Legend characteristics|
|---|---|---|
|Reference map|Shows where things are — cities, borders, roads, terrain|Point, line, and area symbols. Few colors. Emphasis on accuracy.|
|Thematic map|Shows a pattern or relationship — disease rates, income, climate|Color gradients or graduated symbols tied to data. Legend is the primary interpretive tool.|
|Navigation map|Supports movement — road, nautical, aeronautical, trail|Functional symbols optimized for use in motion. Standardized conventions by navigation domain.|

[VERIFY: confirm three-family classification is standard geographic terminology; confirm navigation map as distinct category vs. reference map subset]

### Thematic Maps — Four Sub-types

|Sub-type|What it shows|Legend signal|
|---|---|---|
|Choropleth|Data values by region — shading varies by quantity|Color gradient; each shade = a data range|
|Proportional symbol|Quantity at a point — symbol size varies with data value|Size key showing what each symbol diameter represents|
|Dot density|Distribution of a phenomenon across an area|Each dot = N units; density = concentration|
|Flow map|Movement between places — migration, trade, traffic|Arrow width = volume or quantity of flow|

[VERIFY: confirm sub-type list is sufficient for 9th-grade geography; confirm choropleth spelling and definition]

### The Legend as Argument

Every legend reflects choices. What gets a symbol? What gets left off? How are data ranges divided in a choropleth? A cartographer who divides income data into five equal ranges tells a different story than one who divides the same data at natural breaks. The legend encodes those choices — and a critical map reader notices them.

This connects to GEO-MS-001: a map is always an argument, and the legend is where the argument is made visible. [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Map as argument]

## Section 6 — Worked Example

**ARCS: Confidence · Gagné Event 5 · Procedural + Conceptual-Principle**

Scenario: A student is given a choropleth map showing global internet access rates by country. Legend: five shading categories — 0–20%, 21–40%, 41–60%, 61–80%, 81–100%. Question: which region has the most uneven distribution?

**Step 1 — Read the legend before reading the map.** The legend tells us this is a choropleth: shading = percentage of population with internet access. Darker = higher access. This is a thematic map — it shows a pattern, not locations.

**Step 2 — Identify the pattern question.** "Most uneven distribution" means neighboring countries with very different shading — not where access is highest or lowest overall.

**Step 3 — Apply the legend to compare.** Southeast Asia shows dramatic variation within a small area: Singapore at 81–100%, Cambodia at 21–40%, Myanmar at 21–40%, Timor-Leste at 0–20% — all within close geographic proximity. [VERIFY: confirm internet access data is approximately accurate; confirm Timor-Leste range]

**Step 4 — Answer using legend evidence, not impressions.** The answer is Southeast Asia — because the legend-backed data shows the largest range of shading categories within the smallest geographic region. The legend made that comparison precise.

**What the worked example demonstrates:** The legend is not a reference tool — it is an analytical tool.

### Q1 [NEW] [Bloom: Remember] [Difficulty: Foundational]

A map legend shows: a filled circle, a dashed line, a green shaded area, and a circle that gets larger from left to right. Which symbol type encodes quantity at a specific location?

- A) The filled circle
- B) The dashed line
- C) The green shaded area
- D) The circle that gets larger ✓

**Misconception (D correct):** Graduated symbols encode quantity — size is the variable. Students selecting A confuse point symbols (discrete location) with graduated symbols (quantity at location). Students selecting C confuse area symbols with quantity encoding.

---

### Q2 [NEW] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

A student is given an unfamiliar map. The legend shows: blue lines (rivers), red lines (national borders), brown lines (contour lines at 100m intervals), yellow areas (desert). The student wants to find the highest elevation point. Which legend element is most directly useful?

- A) Blue lines (rivers)
- B) Red lines (national borders)
- C) Brown lines (contour lines at 100m intervals) ✓
- D) Yellow areas (desert)

**Misconception (C correct):** Elevation is encoded by contour lines, not terrain color or political features. Students selecting D may assume "highest" relates to terrain character rather than measured elevation. Students selecting A conflate drainage with elevation.

---

### Q3 [NEW] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

A map shows streets, building footprints, park boundaries, and labeled points of interest — no data values, gradients, or statistical encodings. Which map type?

- A) Thematic map
- B) Navigation map
- C) Reference map ✓
- D) Choropleth map

**Misconception (C correct):** Reference maps show location and feature — no data encoding. Students selecting A assume any detailed map is thematic. D is a thematic sub-type, not a map family — selecting it reveals family/sub-type confusion.

### Q4 [NEW] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

A map of global wheat production shows circles of different sizes over each major wheat-producing country. Legend: small circle = under 10 million tonnes; medium = 10–50 million; large = over 50 million. What type of thematic map?

- A) Choropleth map
- B) Dot density map
- C) Flow map
- D) Proportional symbol map ✓

**Misconception (D correct):** Symbol size tied to quantity at a location = proportional symbol. Students selecting A confuse shaded regions with sized symbols. Students selecting B confuse dot = one unit with symbol = quantity.

### Q5 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Map as argument] [Bloom: Analyze] [Difficulty: Standard]

Two choropleth maps show income inequality in the same country using the same data. Map A uses equal quintile ranges. Map B uses natural statistical breaks, placing 80% of the population in the bottom two categories. A student claims Map B is misleading. A second says Map B is more accurate. Who is right?

- A) The first student — Map B uses an unfair division to exaggerate inequality
- B) The second student — Map B shows where most people actually fall in the data
- C) Neither — both maps are equally valid representations of the same data
- D) Both students are partially right — the division method is a cartographic argument, and both maps are accurate but emphasize different aspects of the same data ✓

**Misconception (D correct):** Both are accurate and both are arguments — the division method encodes a perspective. Students selecting A or B apply a verdict where a trade-off exists. [VERIFY: confirm natural breaks (Jenks) vs. equal interval distinction is appropriate for 9th grade]

### Q6 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-002 — Fitness for purpose] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

A journalist writing about hurricane evacuation routes needs a map showing road types, distances between towns, and major landmarks for readers unfamiliar with the region. Which map type?

- A) Choropleth map showing population density
- B) Flow map showing historical storm tracks
- C) Reference map with road network, distances, and labeled towns ✓
- D) Dot density map showing residential areas

**Misconception (C correct):** The task requires location and navigation information — a reference map. Thematic maps encode data patterns but do not prioritize the navigational detail required.

### Q7 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-003 — Reading coordinates] [Bloom: Apply] [Difficulty: Standard]

A thematic map shows average annual rainfall. Legend: light blue = under 250mm, medium blue = 250–1000mm, dark blue = over 1000mm. A student identifies a location at approximately 5°N, 20°E and notices it falls in the dark blue zone. What can the student conclude?

- A) The location receives over 1000mm of rainfall annually, based on the legend ✓
- B) The location is in a tropical rainforest — dark blue means jungle
- C) The location is near the equator, so it must be humid
- D) The location receives less than 250mm — dark blue is typically drier

**Misconception (A correct):** Conclusion must come from the legend, not assumed associations. Students selecting B import an outside inference not supported by this legend. Students selecting C apply the latitude-climate pattern from GEO-MS-003 as a substitute for reading the legend. Students selecting D have reversed the color encoding. [VERIFY: confirm 5°N, 20°E falls in Central African Republic; confirm approximate rainfall for that region]

### Q8 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-004 — Projection selection for purpose] [Bloom: Analyze] [Difficulty: Challenge]

A thematic map uses a Mercator projection to show global deforestation rates by country — darker shading = higher rates. A critic argues the map overstates deforestation in Canada and Russia and understates it in Brazil and Indonesia. The critic is most likely responding to:

- A) A flaw in the deforestation data itself
- B) The way the choropleth shading was divided — the ranges are unfair
- C) Mercator's area distortion — it inflates the apparent size of high-latitude countries, making their shading appear more dominant than area-proportional maps would show ✓
- D) A political bias in the map's design

**Misconception (C correct):** Mercator inflates area at high latitudes — Canada and Russia appear far larger relative to Brazil and Indonesia. A thematic map on a Mercator base encodes this distortion into the visual argument. Students selecting B apply legend-as-argument correctly but to the wrong mechanism. [VERIFY: confirm Mercator area inflation is meaningfully visible at country scale for Canada/Russia vs. Brazil/Indonesia]

### Q9 [SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Map as argument] [Bloom: Analyze] [Difficulty: Challenge]

A map titled "Access to Clean Water — Global, 2023" uses dot density — each dot = 10,000 people with access. Dense coverage in Western Europe and North America, sparse in Sub-Saharan Africa. A second student points out the map has no legend entry for population density — only for access. What does this observation reveal?

- A) The map is incorrect — it should show population, not access
- B) The map is misleading because high dot density could reflect either high access rates or simply high total population — the legend cannot distinguish between them ✓
- C) The legend is complete — dot density maps always show absolute counts, which is appropriate
- D) The second student is wrong — a dot density map does not need a population legend to be accurate

**Misconception (B correct):** A dot density map of access without a population density control cannot distinguish "many people have access" from "many people live here." The missing legend element — population denominator — is what makes the map incomplete for its stated purpose. Students selecting C or D are not recognizing the structural gap. [VERIFY: confirm this is a recognized critique of dot density maps without population normalization]

## Section 10 — Big Idea Callback (Active — EIB)

> What's the big idea here? Your local government has commissioned a map to show residents which neighborhoods have the highest rates of childhood asthma. You've been asked to advise the cartographer.
> 
> Walk through it:
> 
> 1. Which map type would you recommend — and why does the purpose of the map drive that choice?
> 2. What would the legend need to include for a resident to read this map accurately — and what would make it misleading if left out?
> 3. The cartographer proposes using a Mercator base map. Would you advise keeping it or changing it — and does the projection choice matter for this purpose?
> 
> There's no single right answer. The reasoning is the evidence.

**EIB Rubric (7 points — mastery at 5/7):**

- Names choropleth (or proportional symbol) as appropriate type + justifies with purpose (2 pts)
- Identifies at least two legend elements required for accurate reading (1 pt)
- Identifies at least one element whose absence would be misleading — population denominator or data range method (2 pts)
- Addresses projection question with reasoning — whether Mercator matters depends on whether the map is comparing rates across areas of very different size (2 pts)

## Section 12 — Misconception Inventory

|Misconception|Description|
|---|---|
|All blue = water|Students import prior color associations. This legend overrides universal conventions.|
|Thematic = detailed|Students confuse visual complexity with thematic function. Purpose, not complexity, defines the type.|
|Legend is optional if the map looks familiar|The most common map literacy failure. The legend always takes precedence over recognition.|
|Darker = worse / lighter = better|Color gradient conventions vary. Students must read the legend, not the color intuition.|
|Dot density = accuracy|More dots ≠ more accurate. Dot density maps are rate-blind without a population control.|
|Projection choice is irrelevant to thematic maps|Area distortion on Mercator affects the visual weight of thematic encodings.|
