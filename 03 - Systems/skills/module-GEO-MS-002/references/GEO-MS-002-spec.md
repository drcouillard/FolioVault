## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:**
Scale is not just a measuring tool — it is a choice that determines what a map can and cannot show. No single scale serves every purpose.

**Acceptable Evidence:**
A student can use a scale bar to calculate real-world distance between two points, explain the counterintuitive large/small scale distinction, and select an appropriate scale for a described mapping task — connecting scale choice to the constraint it places on visible detail.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

Scale tells you the relationship between a distance on a map and the corresponding distance in the real world. A map at 1:100,000 means every 1 centimeter on the map represents 100,000 centimeters — 1 kilometer — on the ground. [VERIFY: unit conversion accuracy]

Scale appears in three forms. A **ratio** (1:100,000) states the relationship as a pure number. A **verbal statement** ("one centimeter equals one kilometer") translates that ratio into plain language. A **scale bar** draws the relationship as a physical line on the map — the most practical format, because it stays accurate even when the map is photocopied or resized.

Here is where map vocabulary runs counter to intuition: a **large-scale map** shows a *small* area in high detail. A **small-scale map** shows a *large* area in low detail. The "large" and "small" refer to the representative fraction — 1:10,000 is a larger fraction than 1:1,000,000 — not to the size of the area shown. This reversal trips up almost every new map reader. Name it directly and move past it.

Scale is also a constraint. A map at 1:1,000,000 cannot show individual buildings — there isn't enough resolution. A map at 1:1,000 cannot show an entire country — it would require a map the size of a football pitch. Scale selection is a design decision: what level of detail does this task require, and what area does it need to cover?

## Section 5 — Worked Example

**The scenario:** A navigator needs to estimate the sailing distance from Lisbon, Portugal to Halifax, Canada using a map with a scale bar.

**Step 1 — Read the scale bar.**
The scale bar shows that 2 centimeters on the map equals 1,000 kilometers in reality. The per-unit value is: 1 cm = 500 km.

**Step 2 — Measure the map distance.**
The navigator measures the straight-line distance between Lisbon and Halifax on the map: approximately 6 centimeters.

**Step 3 — Calculate.**
6 cm × 500 km/cm = 3,000 km.

**Step 4 — Evaluate the answer.**
The actual great-circle distance from Lisbon to Halifax is approximately 5,400 km. [VERIFY: Lisbon–Halifax great-circle distance] The calculated answer is lower because the map distance was measured as a straight line on a flat map — not along the curved surface of the Earth. For navigation purposes, the scale bar gives a useful estimate, but the navigator knows to account for the distortion.

That last step — asking whether the answer is reasonable — is as important as the calculation. Scale gives you a number. Judgment tells you whether to trust it.

## Section 6 — Before the Questions

*Placed after worked example, before practice set. Not a numbered section — visual break.*

Here's what this module covers:

- Map scale is the ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding real-world distance — expressed as a ratio, a verbal statement, or a scale bar
- Large-scale maps show small areas in high detail; small-scale maps show large areas in low detail — the vocabulary runs counter to what "large" and "small" suggest
- Scale bars are the most practical format — use them by finding the per-unit value, then multiplying by the measured distance
- Scale is a choice: selecting the wrong scale for a task means the information you need may not be visible at that resolution

> What's the big idea here? Scale isn't a decoration in the corner of a map. It's the contract between the map and reality — the rule that says how much the map has compressed the world to fit on the page. Change the scale and you change what the map can show. There's no scale that shows everything. There's only the scale that's right for the task.

**Q1** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Scale definition and ratio]`

A map has a scale of 1:250,000. What does this scale tell you?

A) The map is 250,000 centimeters wide
B) Every 1 unit on the map represents 250,000 of the same units in reality ✓
C) The map covers an area of 250,000 square kilometers
D) The map was drawn 250,000 years ago

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The scale ratio doesn't describe the map's physical dimensions — it describes the relationship between map distance and real-world distance. A 1:250,000 scale could appear on a map the size of a postage stamp or a poster. What does the ratio 1:250,000 actually tell you about measurement?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Scale ratios describe linear distance relationships, not area. A 1:250,000 scale means one unit of length on the map equals 250,000 of the same length units in reality — it doesn't specify what area the map covers. What does "1 unit to 250,000 units" describe?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Scale ratios have nothing to do with when a map was made. The number 250,000 in a scale ratio is a distance multiplier, not a date. What relationship does the ratio 1:250,000 define?

**Q2** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Scale bar calculation]`

A map's scale bar shows that 4 centimeters equals 200 kilometers. A student measures the distance between two cities on the map as 10 centimeters.

What is the real-world distance between the cities?

A) 250 km
B) 400 km
C) 500 km ✓
D) 800 km

*Calculation: 4 cm = 200 km → 1 cm = 50 km → 10 cm × 50 km = 500 km*

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
That answer divides the map distance by the scale bar distance (10 ÷ 4 = 2.5, then multiplied by something). Work through the unit rate first: if 4 centimeters equals 200 kilometers, how many kilometers does 1 centimeter represent? Then multiply by 10.

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
That answer doubles the scale bar distance (200 × 2 = 400) without accounting for the map distance correctly. The map distance is 10 centimeters, not 2 centimeters. Find the per-centimeter value first, then scale it up to 10 centimeters.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
That answer multiplies the scale bar distance by the map distance directly (200 × 4 = 800 — or a similar error). The scale bar tells you what 4 centimeters equals, not what 1 centimeter equals. What's the per-unit value, and what do you do with it?

**Q3** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Scale bar calculation]`

A hiker is planning a trail route. The map's scale bar shows that 1 centimeter equals 2 kilometers. She measures three trail segments:

- Segment A: 3.5 cm
- Segment B: 2 cm
- Segment C: 4.5 cm

What is the total trail distance in kilometers?

A) 10 km
B) 18 km
C) 20 km ✓
D) 22 km

*Calculation: (3.5 + 2 + 4.5) = 10 cm total → 10 cm × 2 km/cm = 20 km*

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
That answer gives the total map distance in centimeters (10 cm) without converting to real-world distance. You've correctly added the segments — now apply the scale. If 1 centimeter equals 2 kilometers, what does 10 centimeters equal?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Check the addition of the segments: 3.5 + 2 + 4.5. Then apply the scale bar conversion. The per-unit rate is 1 cm = 2 km.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Close — recheck the segment addition. Add 3.5, 2, and 4.5 carefully, then multiply by the per-unit rate (2 km per centimeter).

**Q4** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Large vs. small scale distinction]`

A cartographer describes two maps:
- Map X has a scale of 1:10,000
- Map Y has a scale of 1:2,000,000

Which statement correctly describes the relationship between these maps?

A) Map X is a small-scale map that shows a large area with low detail
B) Map X is a large-scale map that shows a small area with high detail ✓
C) Map Y is a large-scale map because it covers a larger area
D) Both maps show the same level of detail, just at different sizes

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
This is the counterintuitive part of scale vocabulary. A larger fraction (1:10,000 is larger than 1:2,000,000 as a fraction) means large-scale — which means more detail over a smaller area. The word "large" refers to the fraction size, not the area shown.

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Covering a larger area doesn't make a map large-scale — it makes it small-scale. 1:2,000,000 is a smaller fraction than 1:10,000, so Map Y is the small-scale map. Large-scale maps zoom in; small-scale maps zoom out.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Scale directly determines detail level. At 1:10,000, individual buildings and street widths can be shown. At 1:2,000,000, entire countries appear as small patches. These maps show very different levels of detail — the scale ratio controls exactly how much.

**Q5** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Scale as constraint on detail]`

A city planner needs a map that shows the location of every fire hydrant in a neighborhood. She has two maps available:

- Map A: Scale 1:1,000 (covers six city blocks)
- Map B: Scale 1:500,000 (covers the entire metropolitan region)

Why is Map B unsuitable for this task?

A) Map B is too old to show current hydrant locations
B) Map B's small scale means individual hydrants would not be visible at that resolution ✓
C) Fire hydrant locations are classified and cannot appear on regional maps
D) Map B would need to be printed too large to use

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The problem isn't the map's age — it's its scale. A 1:500,000 map compresses reality so much that objects as small as fire hydrants can't be represented as distinct points. What does the scale ratio tell you about how much detail this map can show?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Fire hydrant locations are public infrastructure — they appear on utility maps and emergency planning documents regularly. The issue isn't classification. Think about what 1:500,000 compression means for small objects. At that scale, what's the smallest feature that can be meaningfully represented?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Scale doesn't determine print size — a 1:500,000 map can be printed on a standard sheet of paper. The problem is resolution, not physical size. At 1:500,000, the real world is compressed by a factor of 500,000. What happens to something the size of a fire hydrant under that compression?

**Q6** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Scale selection for purpose]`

A geographer needs to map the distribution of bird species across an entire continent. Which scale would be most appropriate?

A) 1:5,000 — shows individual trees and field boundaries
B) 1:50,000 — shows roads, towns, and land use patterns
C) 1:5,000,000 — shows entire countries and major geographic regions ✓
D) 1:500 — shows individual buildings and property boundaries

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
At 1:5,000, you'd need thousands of separate map sheets to cover a continent. More importantly, showing individual trees would obscure the continental-scale pattern you're trying to see. What scale range allows you to see an entire continent on a manageable map?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
1:50,000 is a useful scale for regional planning — it shows towns and roads clearly. But to show an entire continent, you'd still need hundreds of map sheets, and the detail level is finer than the task requires. Think about the relationship between scale and the geographic unit being mapped.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
At 1:500, a single sheet of paper covers an area roughly the size of a large room. Mapping a continent at this scale would require millions of sheets. The scale needs to match the scope of the task. What scale compresses the world enough to show a whole continent clearly?

**Q7** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Scale definition and ratio]`

A student says: "A map with a scale of 1:1,000,000 is more accurate than a map with a scale of 1:10,000 because it covers more of the world."

What is wrong with this reasoning?

A) Nothing — maps that cover more area are always more accurate
B) Scale and accuracy are unrelated — all maps are equally accurate regardless of scale
C) The student has confused coverage with accuracy — a larger-scale map (1:10,000) shows more detail per unit area, which typically means greater local accuracy ✓
D) The student is correct that 1:1,000,000 is a larger scale than 1:10,000

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Coverage and accuracy are different things. A map that covers more area does so by compressing more — which means each point on the map represents a larger area of the real world, and small features disappear. More coverage means less local detail, not more accuracy.

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Scale and accuracy are related — they're just not related in the way the student described. A 1:10,000 map can show the width of a road accurately. A 1:1,000,000 map cannot — the road would be thinner than a hair at that scale. What does scale do to the level of detail a map can represent?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
1:10,000 is actually the larger scale — it's a larger fraction (1 divided by 10,000 is bigger than 1 divided by 1,000,000). The student has the vocabulary reversed. Large-scale maps show small areas in detail; small-scale maps show large areas with less detail.

**Q8** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Scale as constraint on detail]`

A journalist wants to create two maps for an article about urban food deserts — neighborhoods with limited access to grocery stores. She needs:

- **Map 1:** Show which specific city blocks have no grocery stores within walking distance
- **Map 2:** Show how the pattern compares across all major cities in the country

Which pair of scales is most appropriate?

A) Map 1: 1:500,000 · Map 2: 1:10,000
B) Map 1: 1:10,000 · Map 2: 1:500,000 ✓
C) Map 1: 1:10,000 · Map 2: 1:10,000
D) Map 1: 1:500,000 · Map 2: 1:500,000

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The scales are reversed. Map 1 needs to show individual city blocks — that requires high detail, which means a large-scale map (small ratio number). Map 2 needs to show the whole country — that requires showing a large area, which means a small-scale map (large ratio number). Which ratio is larger: 1:10,000 or 1:500,000?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Using the same scale for both maps would make one of them wrong for its task. At 1:10,000, you'd need thousands of separate maps to cover an entire country — and you still couldn't see national patterns. A single scale can't serve two tasks with this much difference in geographic scope.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
At 1:500,000, individual city blocks are too small to be visible — you'd lose the detail Map 1 requires. And at 1:500,000, showing the whole country is still impossible on a single sheet. Think about what each map needs to show, and match the scale to the scope of each task separately.

**Q9** `[SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Fitness for purpose]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

A search-and-rescue team is looking for a missing hiker in a remote mountain range. They have two maps:

- Map A: 1:25,000 topographic map showing trails, elevation contours, and terrain features of the search area
- Map B: 1:2,000,000 regional map showing the mountain range and surrounding provinces

For the active search — locating the hiker within the terrain — which map serves the team's immediate purpose, and why?

A) Map B — it shows the full extent of the mountain range, giving the team a complete picture
B) Map A — it shows the terrain detail needed to navigate and search effectively within the area ✓
C) Both maps are equally useful — the team should use them simultaneously
D) Neither map — search and rescue teams use GPS, not paper maps

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Map B gives the team context — they can see where the range sits relative to surrounding areas. But context doesn't help them navigate a specific trail or identify a ridge where the hiker might shelter. For the active search, which map shows the terrain at the resolution the team can actually use on the ground?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Both maps have roles — Map B for coordination and communication with outside teams, Map A for the search itself. But the question asks about the immediate task: locating a hiker within the terrain. Which map gives the team actionable detail for that specific task?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
GPS confirms location — but it doesn't show terrain, trails, or the features that affect where a missing person might be found or where searchers can safely travel. Maps and GPS serve different functions. Which map gives the team the geographic detail they need to search effectively?

**Q10** `[SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Map as choice]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

A cartographer is designing a map of a city's cycling infrastructure. She chooses a scale of 1:20,000 and decides to include bike lanes, cycling paths, and repair stations — but omit building footprints, property boundaries, and subway lines.

A colleague says: "You're leaving out too much. This map is incomplete."

Which response best evaluates the colleague's criticism?

A) The colleague is correct — a useful map should include all available information
B) The colleague is incorrect — the omissions are deliberate choices that serve the map's specific purpose ✓
C) The colleague is correct — subway lines are always relevant to cycling maps
D) The colleague is incorrect — maps are not allowed to show subway lines and cycling infrastructure together

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
A map that includes all available information would be unreadable — every detail competes with every other detail. Useful maps make deliberate choices about what to include based on their purpose. What is this map designed to help cyclists do, and does any of the omitted information serve that purpose?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Subway lines might be relevant to a multimodal transit map — but this map is specifically about cycling infrastructure. Including subway lines could be useful as context, but omitting them is a reasonable design decision, not an error. What's the test for whether information belongs on a specific map?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
There's no rule like that — transit maps regularly show multiple transportation modes together. The cartographer's decision to omit subway lines is a purposeful choice for this specific map, not a prohibition. What makes an omission reasonable versus unreasonable on a purpose-built map?
