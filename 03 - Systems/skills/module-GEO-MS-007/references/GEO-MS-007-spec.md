## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:** Spatial thinking is not a single skill — it is the practiced ability to recognize which tools apply to a geographic problem and how to use them in combination. Every map skill introduced in Chapter 1 is partial on its own. The geographic reasoner is the one who notices what a problem requires, selects the right tools, and uses them in concert. This module asks the student to demonstrate that they have built that habit of mind.

**Acceptable Evidence:** A student can: (1) read a complex geographic problem and identify which Chapter 1 tools are required; (2) use multiple tools in combination to extract information from an unfamiliar map; (3) evaluate competing approaches to a spatial reasoning task and justify a choice using map evidence; (4) recognize when a tool is being misused or applied to the wrong kind of problem. Capstone evidence is the EIB.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

### What Spatial Thinking Is

Spatial thinking is the practiced habit of approaching a geographic problem by asking three questions in order:

1. What does this problem actually require me to know?
2. Which of my tools are relevant — and which are not?
3. In what order do I apply the relevant tools to reach an answer I can defend?

The hardest part is not using a single tool — it is recognizing that a problem is a spatial problem and selecting the right combination of tools.

### The Six Tools — Capstone Inventory

|Module|Tool|What it lets you do|
|---|---|---|
|GEO-MS-001|What maps do (and don't do)|Recognize that every map is an argument|
|GEO-MS-002|Scale and distance|Determine real-world distances; assess scale fitness|
|GEO-MS-003|Latitude and longitude|Locate any point using a physical coordinate system|
|GEO-MS-004|Map projections|Identify what projections preserve and distort|
|GEO-MS-005|Legends, symbols, map types|Read a map's visual language; identify map type from purpose|
|GEO-MS-006|Cardinal & intermediate directions|Communicate spatial relationships precisely|

### Tool Combinations — Where Real Reasoning Happens

|Combination|Reasoning unlocked|
|---|---|
|Coordinates + projection|Distinguishing visual distance from physical distance|
|Legend + projection|Recognizing how projection shapes a thematic map's argument|
|Map type + scale|Knowing whether a map is fit for a task at the right scale|
|Direction + coordinates|Stating direction of travel using unambiguous reference|
|Map as argument + every other tool|Reading a map critically — recognizing decisions encoded throughout|

### The Spatial Reasoning Habit

A student who has internalized Chapter 1 has built a habit: when a geographic question arrives, they unpack what it requires, identify the tools that apply, and use them together. The habit is what this module assesses — not recall of any individual tool.

> What's the big idea here? Spatial thinking is not what any one tool does — it's what happens when you use the right tools together for the problem in front of you. A coordinate without a projection is unanchored. A direction without a reference is ambiguous. A legend without a map type is decoration. The geographic reasoner is not the one who knows the most tools. It's the one who notices what the problem requires and reaches for the combination that answers it.

## Section 6 — Worked Example

**ARCS: Confidence · Gagné Event 5 · Conceptual-Principle synthesis**

Scenario: A research team in Greenland has lost contact with a survey crew. Last known position: "approximately 70°N, 40°W, traveling northwest from base camp." Base camp: 69°N, 50°W. Available maps: search-and-rescue map of Greenland on Polar Stereographic projection, 1:1,000,000 scale; global Mercator reference map.

**Step 1 — Identify which tools the problem requires.** Coordinate reading (locate position), directional reasoning (interpret "northwest"), projection assessment (which map fits high latitude?), scale reading (1:1,000,000), map type reasoning (search-and-rescue = navigation; global = reference).

**Step 2 — Locate using coordinates.** 70°N, 40°W. North of base camp by 1° latitude (~111 km). East by 10° longitude — but at 70°N, 10° of longitude is much less than 10° of latitude in actual distance (~38 km). [VERIFY: confirm degree-of-longitude distance at 70°N]

**Step 3 — Interpret the directional information.** "Northwest from base camp" — but the position is north and east of base camp. Direction reversed at some point, or report is inconsistent. Spatial reasoning surfaces this contradiction; it does not paper over it.

**Step 4 — Choose the right map.** At 70°N, Mercator inflates area dramatically — not fit for distance reasoning. Polar Stereographic, centered on the pole, is fit for high-latitude regions. 1:1,000,000 is appropriate for regional search planning. Polar Stereographic search-and-rescue map is the right tool. [VERIFY: confirm Polar Stereographic is standard for high-latitude search-and-rescue]

**Step 5 — Recommendation.** Begin search ~111 km north and ~38 km east of base camp. Flag the directional inconsistency in the report. Use Polar Stereographic for all distance/route reasoning.

**Key demonstration:** Every Chapter 1 tool was used. None was sufficient alone. The reasoning chain is the evidence — not any individual answer.

## Section 10 — Big Idea Callback (Active — EIB)

**Three-scenario student choice. Same rubric across all three.**

### Scenario A — Expedition / Hiking Planning

Five-day backcountry traverse through Andes / Himalayas / Alaska Range (student picks one). Plan route, identify resupply, prepare for weather contingencies.

1. Map type(s) and scale — why is that scale fit for the task?
2. Why does projection matter for high-altitude or high-latitude planning? Which projection family?
3. How do coordinates and direction communicate route to base contact?
4. What is one map-as-argument choice the cartographer made — and how does it affect planning?

### Scenario B — Urban Development

Coastal city planning new transit (light rail / BRT / pedestrian network — pick one).

1. Which map types are essential — and what does each reveal that others don't?
2. Why is scale a critical decision in urban planning? How does it interact with map type?
3. How does projection affect comparing distances and travel times?
4. Population density choropleth — what must the legend include to be used correctly? What absence would mislead?

### Scenario C — Disaster Response (Hurricane Evacuation)

Atlantic or Gulf coast hurricane. 36 hours. Evacuate three coastal counties to inland shelters.

1. Which combination of map types — and why is no single map sufficient?
2. How will coordinates and directional notation communicate routes/shelters across multiple agencies?
3. Why does scale choice directly affect fitness for purpose at each phase?
4. Flood risk choropleth — one decision that compounds usefulness; one that undermines it.

## Section 12 — Misconception Inventory

|Misconception|Description|
|---|---|
|Spatial thinking = using a tool well|Spatial thinking is recognizing that a problem requires multiple tools and selecting the right combination. Tool competence is necessary but not sufficient.|
|Projection is the only thing that distorts|Map type, legend, aggregation level can distort just as much. Spatial reasoning weighs all of them.|
|Coordinates are sufficient for navigation|Coordinates without projection awareness can mislead — visual distance is projection-dependent.|
|One map type is the right map type|Most spatial problems require multiple map types — reference for location, thematic for pattern, navigation for routing.|
|"Mercator is misleading" is a complete argument|It is not. The argument requires identifying which property Mercator distorts and how that distortion interacts with other map decisions.|
|All projections distort, so projection choice doesn't matter|All projections distort, but they distort different properties. The choice of which property is the projection decision.|
