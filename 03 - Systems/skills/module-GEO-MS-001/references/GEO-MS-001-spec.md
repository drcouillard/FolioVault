## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:**
Every map is a decision — a set of choices about what to show, what to hide, and what to distort. A map is not a neutral record of the world. It is an argument about what matters.

**Acceptable Evidence:**
A student can identify what a specific map includes, omits, or distorts — and explain whether those choices make the map appropriate or inappropriate for a given purpose. The student does not need to evaluate every map ever made. They need to demonstrate the reasoning pattern: purpose → choices → fitness.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

Maps make three unavoidable choices. Every map, always.

**Selection** is what the map includes. A political map includes borders and capital cities. It does not include elevation. That's not an error — it's a decision about what matters for the map's purpose. A map that tried to show everything would show nothing clearly.

**Omission** is what the map leaves out. This is selection's other side — and it's worth naming separately because omissions can mislead. A map that shows national parks but not cities gives you a specific picture of a place. It's not lying. But it's not neutral either.

**Distortion** is what the map misrepresents. Every flat map distorts the curved surface of the Earth — area, shape, distance, or direction, always at least one. Some distortions are small enough not to matter. Some aren't. The Mercator projection makes Greenland look roughly the size of Africa. [VERIFY: Mercator Greenland/Africa comparison — confirm relative sizes at standard scale] In reality, Africa is about fourteen times larger. [VERIFY: Africa/Greenland size ratio]

These three choices — selection, omission, distortion — mean that every map is an argument. It argues that these things matter, those things don't, and this is how they relate. A good reader of maps asks: what argument is this map making, and does that argument serve my purpose?

## Section 5 — Worked Example

**The scenario:** Two maps of the same city. One is a subway map. One is a street map.

The subway map shows lines, stops, and connections. It omits surface streets, building footprints, and distances between stops — the lines aren't drawn to scale; a short line might represent a longer distance than a long one. That's a deliberate distortion. It makes the map easier to read for its purpose: navigating the subway system.

The street map shows every street, most buildings, and accurate relative distances. It omits subway lines entirely — or marks them as a thin overlay. For someone driving, this map is exactly right. For someone navigating the subway, it's overwhelming and incomplete.

Neither map is wrong. Both distort. Both omit. Both select. The question is always: **what is this map for?**

A student who can answer that question for any map they encounter has the core skill this module is designed to build.

Here's what this module covers:

- Maps are selective representations — every map makes choices about what to include, what to omit, and what to distort
- Those choices reflect a purpose — a map is appropriate or inappropriate for a task, not simply accurate or inaccurate
- Three unavoidable map choices: selection (what's included), omission (what's left out), distortion (what's misrepresented)
- The right question to ask of any map: what is this map for — and does it make the choices that serve that purpose?

**Q1** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Selection]`

A student is planning a hiking trip and downloads a topographic map of the trail area. The map shows elevation contours, trail markers, and water sources. It does not show nearby towns, roads, or bus routes.

Which statement best describes why the map omits towns and roads?

A) The mapmaker made an error and forgot to include them
B) The map was designed for a specific purpose, and towns and roads are not relevant to that purpose ✓
C) Towns and roads are too difficult to represent on a map
D) Topographic maps are not allowed to show human features

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
That answer treats the missing information as a mistake. Maps are designed with a purpose — what's left out is usually as intentional as what's included. Notice what the topographic map *does* show: elevation, trails, water. Those choices point toward a specific use. What use is that?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Maps do show roads and towns all the time — think of any street map or road atlas. The question isn't whether it's *possible* to include them. It's whether including them serves the map's purpose. What is this map designed to help someone do?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
There's no rule like that — topographic maps of national parks routinely show visitor centers, roads, and trailheads. The reason this map omits them is more specific to its purpose. Look at what the map *does* include. What task is it built for?

**Q2** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Omission]`

A political map of South America shows national borders and capital cities. A student notices that the Amazon rainforest — one of the largest ecosystems on Earth — is not labeled or marked anywhere on the map.

What does this omission tell you?

A) The mapmaker didn't know the Amazon rainforest existed
B) Political maps focus on human-made boundaries and centers of government, not physical geography ✓
C) The Amazon is too large to fit on a political map
D) Political maps are incomplete and should always be used alongside physical maps

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The Amazon is one of the most studied and mapped regions on Earth. The omission isn't about knowledge — it's about purpose. Consider what a political map is designed to show. What kinds of features belong on a map of political boundaries?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
The Amazon covers about 40% of South America — it would appear on the map if the mapmaker wanted it there. Size isn't the reason. Think about what a political map is *for*. Does a rainforest boundary serve that purpose?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
That answer assumes all maps should show everything — but no map does. Every map omits something. The more useful question is whether a specific map is appropriate for a specific task. A political map is the right tool for some tasks. What tasks is it built for?

**Q3** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Distortion]`

On the Mercator projection — the world map most commonly seen in classrooms — Greenland appears to be roughly the same size as Africa. In reality, Africa is significantly larger. [VERIFY: Mercator size comparison, Africa/Greenland ratio]

What does this tell you about the Mercator projection?

A) The Mercator projection was drawn incorrectly
B) All world maps exaggerate the size of Greenland
C) The Mercator projection distorts area, especially near the poles ✓
D) Greenland and Africa are actually similar in size

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The Mercator projection was drawn exactly as intended — it preserves accurate shapes and compass directions, which made it valuable for navigation. The size distortion is a consequence of that design choice, not a mistake. Every map projection trades accuracy in one dimension for accuracy in another. What did the Mercator projection trade?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Different projections handle the poles differently. Some projections minimize size distortion near the poles; others exaggerate it. The Mercator is one specific choice with specific trade-offs. What are those trade-offs?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Africa is approximately fourteen times larger than Greenland by area. [VERIFY] The visual similarity on the Mercator projection is the distortion — not the reality. This is exactly the kind of thing map readers need to notice. What causes the Mercator to show them as similar in size?

**Q4** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Fitness for purpose]`

A journalist is writing a story about which countries have the highest rates of deforestation. She needs a map to include with the article.

Which type of map would best serve her purpose?

A) A political map showing national borders and capitals
B) A physical map showing mountain ranges and river systems
C) A thematic map showing deforestation rates by country ✓
D) A road map showing highways and transportation networks

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
A political map does show countries — which is the right unit for this story. But showing *which countries exist* is different from showing *what's happening in those countries*. What additional information does the journalist need that a political map doesn't provide?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Physical features like mountains and rivers are part of the geographic context, but they don't show deforestation rates. The journalist needs data about what's happening to forests, not about the physical landscape underneath them. What kind of map shows data patterns across geographic areas?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Transportation networks don't have a clear relationship to deforestation rates. Think about what the journalist is trying to show her readers. What would make that story clearest — and what kind of map carries that kind of information?

**Q5** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Fitness for purpose]`

A ship's navigator in 1850 needed to plot a course from London to New York. She needed a map where a straight line drawn between two points would give her an accurate compass bearing to follow.

Which type of map was designed for exactly this purpose?

A) A topographic map
B) A Mercator projection ✓
C) A Peters projection
D) A physical map

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Topographic maps are designed to show terrain elevation — they're the right tool for understanding landscape on land. But a navigator in the middle of the ocean needs something different. What does a navigator need a map to preserve accurately?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
The Peters projection preserves area — it shows countries and continents at their correct relative sizes. That's useful for some purposes. But a navigator following a compass bearing needs a different kind of accuracy. What did the Mercator projection preserve that made it useful for navigation?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Physical maps show landforms — mountains, rivers, coastlines. Useful for many things, but not specifically designed for navigational accuracy. Think about what a ship's navigator needs to do with a map. What property of the map matters most for plotting a compass course?

**Q6** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Selection]`

A mapmaker is creating a map of a city to help tourists find restaurants, museums, and transit stops. She has to decide what to include and what to leave out.

Which of the following would be the most reasonable omission for this map?

A) The locations of major museums
B) The names of subway stations
C) The boundaries of individual property lots ✓
D) The locations of tourist information centers

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Museums are exactly what a tourist map is designed to help people find. Omitting them would undermine the map's purpose. Think about who uses this map and what they're trying to do. Which omission would a tourist never notice because it's not something they're looking for?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Transit stops are one of the most common things tourists look for — especially in an unfamiliar city. Omitting them would make the map significantly less useful for its purpose. Which piece of information on the list is least relevant to tourist navigation?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Tourist information centers are directly useful to tourists — that's their purpose. Think about what tourists need from a city map and what they don't need. Which item on the list serves a completely different audience?

**Q7** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Map as argument]`
`[VERIFY: confirm misconception accuracy for distractors A and C — do design choices constitute a map argument in standard geographic education frameworks?]`

Two maps show the same region of the world. Map A shows the region with large, detailed labels for wealthy countries and smaller, harder-to-read labels for lower-income countries. Map B uses equal label sizes regardless of a country's income level.

A student argues that Map A is "just a design choice" and doesn't make an argument about the world.

Which response best evaluates the student's claim?

A) The student is correct — label size is purely aesthetic and carries no meaning
B) The student is incorrect — Map A's design choices reflect and reinforce assumptions about which countries matter ✓
C) The student is correct — only the data on a map makes an argument, not how it's presented
D) The student is incorrect — maps are not allowed to make arguments; they only record facts

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Label size communicates something — it signals which places are considered important enough to be easily read. Design choices are never purely aesthetic when they consistently favor certain places over others. What does it tell a reader when some countries are consistently harder to find on a map?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Data and presentation are both part of a map's argument. A map that shows accurate data but makes it harder to see certain countries is still making a choice about what's worth seeing clearly. Think about what effect Map A's design would have on a reader who didn't already know the region well.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Maps make arguments all the time — through what they include, what they omit, and how they present information. The claim that maps only record facts is exactly what this module pushes back on. Every map is a set of choices. What does Map A's label-size choice communicate?

**Q8** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Omission]`

A student is studying migration patterns in Europe and downloads a map that shows major cities and transportation routes. She notices the map does not show national borders.

She concludes: "This map is missing important information."

Is her conclusion justified?

A) Yes — national borders are always essential information on any map of Europe
B) No — the map may have omitted borders intentionally to emphasize movement patterns across the continent ✓
C) Yes — a map without borders cannot show accurate geographic relationships
D) No — national borders don't exist in Europe because of the European Union

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Borders are essential on some maps for some purposes — but not on every map. A map designed to show how people and goods move across a continent might deliberately omit borders to avoid making political divisions look more significant than geographic flow. What is this map trying to show?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Geographic relationships — distance, direction, proximity — don't depend on national borders. A map can show accurate spatial relationships using coastlines, rivers, cities, and topography. Think about what "missing information" means: missing relative to what purpose?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
EU member states retain their national borders — the EU is a political and economic union, not a merger of territory. But that's not actually the reason the map omits borders. Think about what the map *does* show, and what purpose that choice serves.

**Q9** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Fitness for purpose]`

A farmer in Kenya needs to decide where to plant different crops based on rainfall patterns across her land. She has access to four maps:

- Map 1: Political map of Kenya showing provinces and major cities
- Map 2: Physical map showing elevation and river systems
- Map 3: Thematic map showing average annual rainfall by region
- Map 4: Road map showing highways and rural access routes

Which map best serves her immediate need?

A) Map 1
B) Map 2
C) Map 3 ✓
D) Map 4

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Provincial boundaries and city locations don't tell the farmer where rainfall is highest or lowest on her land. Think about what information directly affects where different crops will grow. Which map carries that specific data?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Elevation and river systems are related to rainfall — higher elevations and areas near rivers often receive more water. But "related to" isn't the same as "shows directly." Which map gives her the rainfall data itself, without requiring her to infer it from other features?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Road access matters for getting crops to market — that's a real consideration. But the question asks about where to *plant*, not how to *transport*. For the planting decision, which map gives her the most direct information?

**Q10** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Map as argument]`

A student looks at a world map in her classroom. She notices that the map places Europe near the center and makes the continents in the Northern Hemisphere appear larger than those in the Southern Hemisphere.

She says: "Maps are objective — they just show the world."

Which response most directly challenges her claim?

A) She's right — maps are scientific documents and therefore objective
B) She's wrong — maps always lie about everything and cannot be trusted
C) Her claim overlooks that map design choices — including what's centered and what's enlarged — reflect decisions that are not neutral ✓
D) She's right — the Northern Hemisphere is actually larger than the Southern Hemisphere

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Maps use measurement and data — but measurement choices are still choices. What gets centered, what gets enlarged, what gets labeled prominently — all of these reflect decisions. A document can use scientific methods and still embed assumptions. What assumptions does this map's design reflect?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
That overcorrects. Maps are genuinely useful tools — this whole course depends on reading them well. The point isn't that maps lie about everything. It's that maps make choices, and those choices aren't neutral. What specific choices does this map make, and what do they communicate?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
The Northern and Southern Hemispheres are equal in area by definition — the equator divides Earth into two equal halves. [VERIFY] The apparent size difference on this map is a product of the projection and centering choices, not a reflection of actual size. What does that tell you about what the map is doing?

## Section 10 — Big Idea Callback (Active — EIB Prompt)

*Placed at end of module, after practice set.*

> What's the big idea here? A city planning office has asked your team to create a map showing which neighborhoods have the highest surface temperatures during summer heatwaves. The map will be used to decide where to plant trees and install shade structures.
>
> Walk through it:
> 1. What would you include on this map — and what would you deliberately leave out?
> 2. Your map will show temperature by neighborhood. What distortions or limitations should readers know about before drawing conclusions?
> 3. Someone looking at your map says: "This map proves these neighborhoods are the most polluted." Is that a fair reading of your map? Why or why not?
>
> There's no single right answer. The reasoning is the evidence.
