## Section 2 — Learning Objectives

**Primary Learning Objective:**
By the end of this module, the learner will be able to analyze the EU's geographic structure — member distribution, Schengen border removal, and economic geography — and explain how physical and historical geography shaped which states joined, when, and why.

**Tutor-Acumen Companion Objective:**
The learner will be able to explain to a peer who believes Brexit was purely a political decision why island geography creates a structurally different relationship to continental integration — identifying at least one specific geographic mechanism.

**Sub-Objectives:**

1. *Apply* — Locate EU founding members and identify the geographic cluster they form. *(Bloom: Apply)*
2. *Analyze* — Explain the geographic logic of each EU expansion wave using physical and economic geography. *(Bloom: Analyze)*
3. *Analyze* — Distinguish Schengen Area membership from EU membership and explain why the two maps differ. *(Bloom: Analyze)*
4. *Evaluate* — Evaluate Brexit as a geographic case study, using island geography as the primary analytic frame. *(Bloom: Evaluate)*

## Section 7 — Concept Explainer

### What the EU Is — Geographically

The European Union is a political and economic union of 27 member states occupying roughly 4.2 million km² in Europe. What makes it geographically significant is not its political structure — it is what it has done to borders. `[VERIFY: current member count and land area]`

For most of European history, the continent's internal borders were military facts — enforced, contested, and frequently redrawn by force. The EU replaced that system with a space in which goods, money, people, and services move freely across member state boundaries. The Schengen Area removed passport controls at internal borders for most member states. `[VERIFY: current Schengen member list]`

### The Founding Geography

The EU's origin is the 1957 Treaty of Rome, which created the European Economic Community among six founding members: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. `[VERIFY: Treaty of Rome date and founding members]`

Look at those six on a map. They cluster in the industrial and agricultural core of Western Europe. Four share the Rhine River basin. All six were occupied or directly affected by WWII. The geographic logic is not subtle: the most conflict-prone corridor of Europe was integrated first. Economic integration made that geography economically interdependent rather than militarily contested.

### Expansion Waves and Their Geographic Logic

The EU did not expand randomly. Each wave followed a geographic and political logic.

*1973 — UK, Ireland, Denmark:* Northern maritime states. The UK's accession was contested for years — France vetoed it twice — partly because island geography creates a different relationship to continental integration. Ireland joined because its primary trading partner (the UK) was joining.

*1980s — Greece, Spain, Portugal:* Mediterranean periphery. All three had recently transitioned from authoritarian governments to democracies. Their geographic position on the southern edge — and economic development gaps with the north — established the pattern of a wealthier western/northern core and a developing southern periphery that still shapes EU economic geography today.

*2004–2013 — Eastern enlargement:* The largest expansion wave. Ten Central and Eastern European states joined in 2004, followed by Bulgaria and Romania in 2007, and Croatia in 2013. This wave followed the collapse of the Soviet bloc — the EU's eastern border moved from the Iron Curtain line to the borders of Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, and the western Balkans.

### The Schengen Area and Its Geographic Shape

The Schengen Agreement removed internal border controls among its signatories. Not all EU members are Schengen members. Ireland and the UK (before Brexit) maintained their own border controls. Some non-EU states — Norway, Iceland, Switzerland, Liechtenstein — are Schengen members.

The differences between the Schengen map and the EU map are themselves geographic. Island nations and states with specific border concerns have consistently negotiated exceptions. `[VERIFY: current Schengen member list]`

### The Eurozone — Economic Geography

19 of the EU's 27 members use the Euro. `[VERIFY: current Eurozone member count]` The geographic pattern of Eurozone membership roughly tracks economic development — the wealthiest and most economically integrated states adopted the common currency; states with weaker economies or stronger national currency traditions have not.

The Rhine corridor — running from Switzerland through Germany and the Netherlands to the North Sea — remains the EU's economic spine. Understanding why requires going back to Module 7.0: navigable rivers, temperate climate, flat terrain, and dense pre-existing settlement. The EU's economic geography is largely a function of physical geography that predates it by centuries.

### Brexit: Island Geography as Political Identity

The UK left the EU on January 31, 2020. `[VERIFY: Brexit date]`

The UK is an island. It was never connected to the North European Plain by land. Its military history is a maritime history — control of the seas rather than management of land borders. Its economic orientation has historically been Atlantic and global rather than continental. When the EU's integration deepened toward common governance, the UK's geographic instinct — reinforced by centuries of deliberate separation from continental entanglements — produced a different political calculation than it did for France or Germany.

This is not a complete explanation of Brexit. It is a geographic frame for understanding why an island nation's relationship to continental integration was always structurally different from a mainland nation's.

## Section 8 — Worked Example

**Reading an EU Membership Map**

*Step 1 — Identify the founding core.* Find France, Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. They cluster in the center-west. This is where the EU started.

*Step 2 — Trace the expansion waves.* Notice how membership expands outward from that core — first north (UK, Denmark), then south (Greece, Spain, Portugal), then east (2004 and 2007 expansion). The EU's geographic footprint grew in concentric waves from its founding center.

*Step 3 — Find the non-members.* Norway and Switzerland are wealthy, Western European, and not members — note the geographic position of both. The Western Balkans are mostly candidate or potential candidate states. Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus are not candidates. The non-member geography tells a political story.

*Step 4 — Overlay Schengen.* Notice that the Schengen zone and EU membership are not identical maps. The difference marks where geographic or political identity produced a different calculation about border integration.

*Step 5 — Ask the geographic question.* Where is the EU's geographic center of gravity? Where are its economic peripheries? Where are its external borders most contested? A map answers all three if you know what to look for.

### Q1 `[NEW: EU Founding Geography]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

- B) The six countries cluster in the industrial and agricultural core of Western Europe, connected by the Rhine River system. ✓
- C) The six countries were chosen because they shared a common language and cultural heritage.
- D) The six countries were the only Western European states not occupied during WWII.

### Q2 `[NEW: Schengen vs. EU Membership]` `[Bloom: Analyze]`

Norway is not a member of the European Union. It is, however, a full member of the Schengen Area, meaning its borders with EU member states have no passport controls.

Switzerland is also a Schengen member but not an EU member.

Explain why the Schengen Area and EU membership are not identical maps. What does the geographic pattern of non-EU Schengen members tell you about how geographic position can shape a state's relationship to European integration?

*Open response. No scaffold.*

**Mastery criterion:** Student must explain that Schengen and EU membership serve different functions (border control vs. full economic/political integration) AND connect Norway's and Switzerland's geographic positions to their Schengen-but-not-EU status. Acceptable arguments: Norway and Switzerland are deeply economically integrated with EU neighbors and benefit from free movement of goods/people; their geographic position within the European core makes border friction costly; but both have specific political or economic reasons for remaining outside full EU membership (Norwegian sovereignty over petroleum resources; Swiss neutrality tradition). Geographic position as a connector between EU states is the key mechanism.

**Diagnostic Feedback:**

```
Wrong answer: Student treats Schengen and EU membership as
              essentially the same thing with minor exceptions
Misconception: Student has not distinguished the different
               functions of Schengen participation vs. EU
               membership — treating both as versions of
               the same integration.
Feedback:      Schengen removes passport controls at borders —
               it is specifically about the physical movement
               of people across geographic boundaries.
               EU membership includes economic policy,
               legal frameworks, currency (for Eurozone members),
               and political representation. A country can
               agree to open borders without agreeing to
               share economic policy. Norway and Switzerland
               have made exactly that calculation. What does
               their geographic position — landlocked in
               Switzerland's case, flanked by EU states in
               Norway's — make practically necessary even
               without full EU membership?
```

### Q3 `[NEW: Brexit Island Geography]` `[Bloom: Evaluate]`

A historian argues: "Brexit was inevitable — not because of politics, but because of geography. Island nations have always had a different relationship to continental integration than mainland states."

Evaluate this argument. Identify one piece of geographic evidence that supports it and one limitation of using geography alone to explain Brexit.

*Open response. No scaffold.*

**Mastery criterion:** Student must engage with the evaluative structure — support AND limitation required, not just one side. Support: UK's island position means no land borders with EU members, maritime economic orientation, centuries of deliberate non-entanglement with continental European conflicts, Channel as a geographic barrier that shaped distinct political identity. Limitation: Ireland is also an island and remained in the EU; geographic determinism overstates physical geography's role; political, economic, and cultural factors shaped the specific timing and form of Brexit in ways geography cannot explain alone. Both sides required for full marks.

**Diagnostic Feedback:**

```
Wrong answer: Student provides only support for the geographic
              argument without addressing its limitations
Misconception: Student has engaged with only half the
               evaluative task — supporting the claim
               without testing it.
Feedback:      You've identified geographic evidence that
               supports the historian's argument. The question
               also asks for a limitation — a place where
               the geographic explanation breaks down.
               Consider: Ireland is also an island, and it
               remained in the EU. Does that complicate the
               claim that island geography makes EU membership
               structurally unlikely? What does Ireland's
               different choice tell you about the limits
               of a purely geographic explanation?
```

### Q6 `[SPIRAL: Human Geography — Migration]` `[Bloom: Analyze]`

The Schengen Agreement's free movement provision allows any EU/Schengen citizen to live and work in any member state without a visa or work permit.

After the 2004 eastern enlargement, significant labor migration occurred from Poland, Romania, and other Eastern European states to Western European countries like the UK, Germany, and Ireland.

Identify one geographic reason this migration pattern followed the direction it did — from east to west — rather than the reverse.

*Open response. No sentence frame.*

**Mastery criterion:** Student must identify an economic geography mechanism — wage and income differentials between eastern and western EU states; proximity of Eastern European states to Western European economic core (North European Plain corridor enabling low-cost movement); Western European labor demand in construction, agriculture, and service sectors. The geographic pattern of migration should be connected to economic geography, not just stated as a fact.

### Q10 `[SPIRAL: 7.0 + 7.1 Synthesis]` `[Bloom: Evaluate]`

The European Union's geographic footprint has expanded significantly since 1957 — from six founding members clustered in Western Europe's industrial core to 27 members spanning the continent.

Evaluate this claim: "The EU's geographic expansion has made it stronger economically but more complex geographically."

Your response should identify one piece of geographic evidence for the economic strengthening argument and one piece of geographic evidence for the increased complexity argument.

*Open response. No scaffold.*

**Mastery criterion:** Economic strengthening: single market expansion providing larger labor pool and consumer market; eastern enlargement adding agricultural land and manufacturing capacity; geographic diversification reducing dependence on Rhine corridor alone. Increased complexity: greater variation in economic development levels across member states creating geographic tension between core and periphery; longer external borders requiring more complex management; greater geographic diversity of strategic interests among members (Baltic states' Russia-facing concerns vs. Mediterranean states' North Africa-facing concerns). Evidence must be geographic — not just "more members = more complex."
