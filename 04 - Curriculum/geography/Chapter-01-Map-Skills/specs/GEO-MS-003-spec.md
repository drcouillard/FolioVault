## Section 2 — Enduring Understanding & Acceptable Evidence

**Enduring Understanding:**
Coordinates are not just a lookup system — they are a reasoning tool. Every location on Earth can be described with two numbers, and those numbers carry geographic meaning before you consult any other source. Latitude tells you something about climate, season length, and solar angle. Longitude tells you something about time zones and east-west position relative to a human agreement made in 1884.

**Acceptable Evidence:**
A student can read a coordinate pair and place it accurately on a map, write the coordinates of a described location in standard format, and use coordinate information to make a geographic inference — without being told what to infer. The student understands that latitude and longitude are different kinds of lines with different physical meanings, not interchangeable axes.

## Section 4 — Concept Explainer

The Earth is a sphere. To describe any location on a sphere, you need two measurements: how far north or south of a reference line, and how far east or west of a reference line.

**Latitude** measures angular distance north or south of the equator. The equator is 0°. The North Pole is 90°N. The South Pole is 90°S. Lines of latitude run east-west and are always parallel to each other — which is why they're also called parallels. The distance between any two lines of latitude is consistent: one degree of latitude equals approximately 111 kilometers on the ground. [VERIFY: 1° latitude = 111 km — confirm standard approximation]

**Longitude** measures angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian. The Prime Meridian is 0° — it runs through Greenwich, England, established by international agreement in 1884. [VERIFY: Prime Meridian convention date] Lines of longitude run north-south and converge at both poles. Unlike latitude lines, longitude lines are not parallel — they spread apart at the equator and meet at the poles. This means the distance represented by one degree of longitude varies: it's about 111 kilometers at the equator and zero at the poles. [VERIFY: longitude distance variation]

Coordinates are always written in the same order: **latitude first, longitude second**. Each value includes a direction — N or S for latitude, E or W for longitude. The coordinate 34°S, 18°E places a point in the southern hemisphere, east of the Prime Meridian — near Cape Town, South Africa. [VERIFY: Cape Town approximate coordinates]

One edge case worth naming: the North Pole has a latitude of 90°N, but no defined longitude. Every line of longitude meets at the poles — so the concept of east or west has no meaning there. This isn't an error in the system. It's a geometric consequence of how meridians work.

A final note, held here deliberately: latitude correlates with climate patterns. Locations near the equator receive more direct solar radiation year-round; locations near the poles receive less and at a lower angle. You can begin to make predictions about a place's climate from its latitude alone — before consulting any other data. The mechanism behind that pattern is Chapter 2's work. For now: notice it.

## Section 5 — Worked Example

**The scenario:** A navigator receives a distress call from a vessel reporting its position as 34°S, 18°E. The navigator needs to identify the region and determine which coast guard authority to contact.

**Step 1 — Read latitude.**
34°S places the vessel in the southern hemisphere, roughly equivalent in distance from the equator as North Africa or the southern United States are in the northern hemisphere. This is a mid-latitude southern location.

**Step 2 — Read longitude.**
18°E places the vessel east of the Prime Meridian — in the eastern hemisphere. Combined with the latitude, this puts the vessel in the southern Africa region.

**Step 3 — Narrow the location.**
34°S, 18°E is near Cape Town, South Africa — specifically in the waters of the South Atlantic Ocean approaching the Cape of Good Hope. [VERIFY: confirm 34°S, 18°E places vessel near Cape Town / South Atlantic]

**Step 4 — Apply the inference.**
The appropriate authority is the South African Maritime Safety Authority (SAMSA), which manages search and rescue operations in that region. [VERIFY: SAMSA jurisdiction at these coordinates]

The navigator did not need a labeled map to begin reasoning. The coordinates alone placed the vessel in a specific ocean, near a specific coast, in a specific country's jurisdiction. That's coordinates as a reasoning tool — not just a lookup.

Latitude is a physical fact — the equator exists because of how Earth sits relative to the sun, and every parallel reflects that geometry. **Q1** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Remember]` `[Sub-objective: Definitions — latitude vs. longitude]`

Which of the following correctly distinguishes latitude from longitude?

A) Latitude measures east-west position; longitude measures north-south position
B) Latitude measures north-south position from the equator; longitude measures east-west position from the Prime Meridian ✓
C) Latitude and longitude both measure distance from the equator, but in different directions
D) Latitude measures distance in kilometers; longitude measures distance in degrees

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The directions are reversed. Latitude lines run east-west across the map — but they measure how far *north or south* you are from the equator. Longitude lines run north-south — but they measure how far *east or west* you are from the Prime Meridian. The direction a line runs and what it measures are different things.

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Longitude doesn't measure distance from the equator — it measures distance from the Prime Meridian, which runs north-south through Greenwich, England. The two coordinates use two different reference lines. What is each one measured from?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Both latitude and longitude are measured in degrees — they're both angular measurements, not linear distances. One degree of latitude does correspond to roughly 111 km on the ground, but that's a conversion, not how the coordinate itself is defined. How are both coordinates expressed?

**Q2** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Physical logic of latitude — parallels]`

Why are lines of latitude called "parallels"?

A) Because they run parallel to the Prime Meridian
B) Because they are always the same length as each other
C) Because they run parallel to the equator and never intersect each other ✓
D) Because they were developed in parallel with lines of longitude

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The Prime Meridian runs north-south — lines of latitude run east-west. They're perpendicular, not parallel. Think about what lines of latitude are parallel *to* — what reference line do they all run alongside?

**Diagnostic feedback — B:**
Lines of latitude are actually different lengths — the equator is the longest, and they get progressively shorter as they approach the poles, where they shrink to a point. They're called parallels because of their relationship to each other, not their length. What geometric property do they share?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
"Parallel" here is a geometric description, not a historical one. Look at any globe or world map — trace a line of latitude, then trace another. What is true about those two lines relative to each other at every point?

**Q3** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Prime Meridian origin]`

Why does the Prime Meridian run through Greenwich, England, rather than through another location?

A) Greenwich is the geographic center of the Earth's landmasses
B) The Prime Meridian was placed at Greenwich by international agreement in 1884, when Britain had the world's largest navy and most widely used nautical charts ✓
C) Greenwich is located exactly halfway between the North and South poles
D) The Prime Meridian must pass through a major capital city, and London was chosen

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The geographic center of Earth's landmasses is not in Greenwich — it's been calculated to be somewhere in Asia or the Middle East depending on the method. [VERIFY: geographic center of landmasses] The Prime Meridian's location wasn't determined by geography. What determined it?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Every line of longitude runs from the North Pole to the South Pole — they all pass exactly halfway between the poles by definition. That can't be what distinguishes Greenwich. What was special about Greenwich in 1884 that led to the agreement?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
The Prime Meridian doesn't need to pass through a capital city — it could run through open ocean if the agreement had gone differently. The choice of Greenwich was about practical influence, not a rule about capitals. What gave Britain the leverage to establish the international standard?

**Q4** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Reading coordinates]`

A ship reports its position as 52°N, 4°E. In which general region is the ship located?

A) South Atlantic Ocean, near the coast of South America
B) North Sea, near the coast of Western Europe ✓
C) Indian Ocean, near the coast of East Africa
D) Arctic Ocean, near Greenland

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
52°N places the ship in the northern hemisphere — South America is in the southern hemisphere for most of its length. And 4°E places the ship east of the Prime Meridian, not in the western Atlantic. Read the two coordinates separately: what does 52°N tell you about hemisphere and distance from the equator?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
4°E is close to the Prime Meridian — the Indian Ocean is much further east, around 60–80°E for its western edge. And 52°N is well north of the equator, not near East Africa's latitude. Place the longitude first: where is 4°E on a world map?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
52°N is not far enough north for the Arctic — the Arctic Circle begins at approximately 66.5°N. [VERIFY: Arctic Circle latitude] At 52°N, you're at roughly the latitude of the United Kingdom, Germany, or southern Canada. Where does 52°N actually sit on the map?

**Q5** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Writing coordinates]`

A researcher wants to mark the location of a field station in the Australian outback. The station is approximately 25 degrees south of the equator and 134 degrees east of the Prime Meridian.

How should she write these coordinates in standard format?

A) 134°E, 25°S
B) 25°S, 134°E ✓
C) 25°N, 134°W
D) S25°, E134°

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The values are correct, but the order is wrong. Coordinates are always written latitude first, longitude second — regardless of which value is larger or which comes first alphabetically. What is the standard order?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
The station is south of the equator — that's S, not N. And 134°E is east of the Prime Meridian — that's E, not W. Both directions are wrong. Re-read the problem: which hemisphere (north or south) and which side of the Prime Meridian (east or west)?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
The values are correct, but the format is wrong. The convention places the degree symbol after the number and the direction after that: 25°S, not S25°. Standard coordinate notation also puts latitude before longitude. What does the correct format look like?

**Q6** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Apply]` `[Sub-objective: Coordinate inference]`

A student is given the coordinate 0°, 0° and asked to describe where it falls.

Which answer demonstrates the strongest geographic reasoning?

A) It's in the middle of the map — probably somewhere important
B) It's where the equator and the Prime Meridian cross — in the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of West Africa ✓
C) It's at the North Pole, where all coordinates meet
D) It's at the center of the Earth, underground

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
"Middle of the map" depends on which map — and even if the coordinate appears near the center of a particular map, that tells you nothing about what's actually there. Use what the numbers mean: 0° latitude and 0° longitude each refer to specific reference lines. Where do those lines cross?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
The North Pole is 90°N — not 0°. And longitude loses meaning at the poles, but 0° latitude is the equator, not a pole. Think about each coordinate separately: what does 0° latitude mean, and what does 0° longitude mean?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Coordinates describe surface locations on Earth — they don't refer to underground positions. The coordinate system is a grid on the surface of the sphere. What surface location does the intersection of 0° latitude and 0° longitude describe?

**Q7** `[NEW]` `[Bloom: Understand]` `[Sub-objective: Meridian convergence]`

At the equator, one degree of longitude represents approximately 111 kilometers. At 60°N latitude, one degree of longitude represents approximately 55 kilometers. At the North Pole, one degree of longitude represents zero kilometers. [VERIFY: longitude distance values at equator, 60°N, and pole]

What explains this pattern?

A) The Earth is wider at the equator than at the poles
B) Lines of longitude converge as they approach the poles, so the distance between them decreases ✓
C) The Earth spins faster at the equator, stretching the longitude lines apart
D) Longitude is measured differently at different latitudes

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
The Earth is slightly wider at the equator — but that's not what explains the pattern in the question. The pattern is about the distance between longitude *lines*, not the size of the Earth itself. Think about what happens to lines of longitude as they travel from the equator toward the poles. What do they do?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Earth's rotation speed doesn't affect how longitude is measured or how far apart the lines are. The pattern is geometric, not mechanical. Visualize lines of longitude on a globe — they start spread apart at the equator. What happens to them as they approach the poles?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Longitude is defined the same way everywhere — it's always the angular distance from the Prime Meridian. What changes is the *linear* distance between longitude lines at different latitudes. Why would the same angular gap represent different distances depending on where you are on the globe?

**Q8** `[SPIRAL: GEO-MS-002 — Scale and distance]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

A map has a scale of 1:1,000,000 and uses a standard coordinate grid. A student measures the map distance between two points at 45°N latitude as 5 centimeters.

She calculates the real-world distance as 50 kilometers. Her classmate says this answer will be slightly different from the actual ground distance.

Who is correct, and why?

A) The student is correct — scale bar calculations always give exact ground distances
B) The classmate is correct — the map projects a curved surface onto a flat plane, so flat-map distances introduce some distortion ✓
C) The classmate is correct — the scale of 1:1,000,000 is too small to give accurate measurements
D) The student is correct — coordinate grids eliminate projection distortion

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Scale bar calculations give accurate results *for the map* — but the map itself is a projection of a curved surface onto a flat plane. That projection introduces distortion, which means flat-map measurements don't perfectly match curved-surface distances. What does projection do to the accuracy of distance measurements?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
1:1,000,000 is a small-scale map, but scale doesn't determine whether measurements are accurate — projection does. Even a large-scale map (like 1:10,000) introduces some projection distortion. The issue isn't the scale ratio; it's the geometry of representing a sphere on a flat surface.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Coordinate grids help you locate points precisely — but they don't eliminate the distortion introduced when a curved surface is projected flat. Projection distortion and coordinate accuracy are different things. What happens to straight-line distances when a sphere is flattened?

**Q9** `[SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Map as argument]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

Two world maps use identical coordinate grids but different projections. On Map A, the lines of longitude appear as straight vertical lines. On Map B, the lines of longitude curve toward each other at the top and bottom of the map.

A student says Map A is more accurate because "straight lines are clearer."

What does this claim overlook?

A) Nothing — straight longitude lines are always more accurate than curved ones
B) Straight longitude lines on a flat map require the projection to distort something else — likely area or shape near the poles ✓
C) Map B is always more accurate because curved lines reflect the Earth's shape
D) The accuracy of a map has nothing to do with how longitude lines are drawn

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Straight longitude lines on a flat map require a trade-off — you can't make curved lines straight without distorting something else. The Mercator projection uses straight longitude lines and pays for it with severe area distortion near the poles. What does the student's claim overlook about the nature of projection trade-offs?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
Map B may reflect the Earth's curved geometry more literally — but that doesn't make it universally more accurate. Both projections distort something; they just distort different things. The question isn't which map has curved lines. It's what each map's design choices cost.

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
Projection choices directly affect accuracy in specific dimensions — area, shape, distance, or direction. How longitude lines are drawn is a consequence of the projection, and the projection determines what the map gets right and what it distorts. What does the shape of longitude lines tell you about what the projection has done?

**Q10** `[SPIRAL: GEO-MS-001 — Fitness for purpose]` `[Bloom: Apply]`

An ecologist is studying temperature patterns across Africa. She wants a map that lets her quickly identify which locations share the same latitude — because latitude correlates with solar radiation patterns she is tracking.

Which map feature would most directly serve her purpose?

A) A map with country borders and capital cities labeled
B) A map with clearly marked parallels at regular intervals ✓
C) A map with longitude lines emphasized and latitude lines removed
D) A map with a detailed scale bar for measuring distances

**Diagnostic feedback — A:**
Country borders and capitals help with political geography — but they don't show where the parallels fall across the continent. The ecologist needs to identify shared latitudes at a glance. Which map feature makes that possible directly?

**Diagnostic feedback — C:**
If the ecologist is tracking latitude-based patterns, removing latitude lines defeats the purpose. Longitude lines show east-west position — not the north-south solar radiation pattern she's studying. Which type of line marks latitude?

**Diagnostic feedback — D:**
A scale bar helps measure distances — but it doesn't help identify which locations share a latitude. The ecologist needs a visual reference for latitude, not a distance measurement tool. What map feature shows latitude directly?

## Section 10 — Big Idea Callback (Active — EIB Prompt)

*Placed at end of module, after practice set.*

> What's the big idea here? A historian argues that the Prime Meridian could have been placed anywhere — that its location in Greenwich is arbitrary, and a coordinate system with 0° longitude running through Paris or Beijing would work just as well.
>
> Walk through it:
> 1. Is the historian's claim technically correct? Would a coordinate system with a different Prime Meridian still function as a coordinate system?
> 2. What would change — practically — if the Prime Meridian ran through a different city? What would stay the same?
> 3. The choice of Greenwich in 1884 wasn't made on geographic grounds — it was made on political and economic ones. Does that make the coordinate system less valid? Explain your reasoning.
>
> There's no single right answer. The reasoning is the evidence.

**EIB Rubric — 7 points, mastery at 5/7**

| Criterion | Points | Mastery indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Correctly evaluates the technical claim | 2 | Student recognizes the system would function regardless of Prime Meridian placement — the reference line is arbitrary but the system is not |
| Identifies what would and wouldn't change with a different Prime Meridian | 2 | Student names specific practical consequences (all longitude values shift) and what remains constant (the system's function, latitude, distances) |
| Distinguishes validity from origin | 2 | Student recognizes that a politically determined convention can still be scientifically valid and universally useful |
| Reasoning is coherent across all three parts | 1 | Answers connect — the conclusion about validity follows from the analysis of what changes and what doesn't |
