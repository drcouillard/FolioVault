MODULE STRUCTURE

Module 1.0 — What Maps Do (and Don't Do)
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-001
  Weight:           Anchor (within chapter)
  Estimated Time:   3–4 sessions
  Content Status:   Not started

  Enduring Understanding:
    Every map is an argument. It shows some things and hides
    others, and every choice a mapmaker makes — what to include,
    what scale to use, what projection to apply — reflects a
    purpose. A student who understands this does not just read
    maps more accurately; they read them more skeptically.
    That skepticism is the foundation of spatial thinking.

  Topic Summary:
    The orientation module for the entire chapter. Before any
    specific map skill is taught, students develop the conceptual
    frame: maps are models of reality, not reality itself. The
    module introduces what maps are for, what they cannot do,
    and why the same place looks different on different maps.
    No coordinate systems, no projections yet — just the
    foundational idea that a map is a designed artifact with
    a purpose.

  Key Concepts:
    - Maps as models — what they represent and what they omit
    - The three things every map requires: scale, orientation,
      and a legend
    - Why the same geographic feature looks different on
      different map types
    - The difference between a physical map, a political map,
      and a thematic map — and when each is the right tool
    - Spatial thinking as a habit of mind: asking "where" and
      "why there" as linked questions

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Remember → Understand
  Prerequisites:          None — this is the course entry point
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2

  Sequencing Note:
    Required first. Students who begin with coordinate systems
    or projections before understanding why maps exist will
    memorize procedures without understanding what they're for.
    The conceptual frame must precede the technical tools.
    This module is short by design — its job is to orient,
    not to cover content.

Module 1.1 — Scale: How Far Is Far?
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-002
  Weight:           Heavy
  Estimated Time:   4–5 sessions
  Content Status:   Partial — POC includes worked example
                    and 2 practice questions. Full module
                    spec not yet built.

  Enduring Understanding:
    Scale is not just a technical feature of maps — it is a
    decision about how much of the world to show and how much
    detail to include. Understanding scale means understanding
    that the same distance means something different depending
    on what you're measuring and why. A student who can use
    a scale bar to calculate real-world distance has a
    geographic tool that applies everywhere.

  Topic Summary:
    Introduces map scale in three forms — verbal scale, ratio
    scale, and graphic/bar scale — and establishes the
    relationship between scale and detail. Students learn to
    calculate real-world distance from a map scale, understand
    why large-scale and small-scale maps look the way they do,
    and begin applying scale reasoning to geographic questions
    (e.g., "how long would this route take?", "how far is this
    city from that border?"). The POC worked example (navigator
    logic using real map scale) lives here.

  Key Concepts:
    - Three forms of scale: verbal ("1 inch = 100 miles"),
      ratio (1:1,000,000), and graphic/bar scale
    - Large scale vs. small scale — the counterintuitive naming
      convention and what it means for detail and coverage
    - Using a scale bar to calculate real-world distance
    - Why scale affects what a map can and cannot show
    - Scale and the appropriate map: matching scale to purpose

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Understand → Apply
  Prerequisites:          Module 1.0 (maps as models)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2

  Sequencing Note:
    Scale is the first technical tool because it is the most
    universally applicable — students will use scale in every
    regional module to calculate distances, compare country
    sizes, and interpret physical geography maps. It also
    has the lowest conceptual threshold of the technical
    tools, making it the right entry point after the
    orientation module.

Module 1.2 — Latitude & Longitude: The Address System of Earth
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-003
  Weight:           Heavy
  Estimated Time:   4–5 sessions
  Content Status:   Partial — POC includes 2 practice questions.
                    Full module spec not yet built.

  Enduring Understanding:
    Latitude and longitude are not arbitrary lines — they are
    a system for describing position on a sphere, and they
    encode climate, time, and place all at once. A student
    who understands how the coordinate system works can locate
    any place on Earth, estimate its climate zone from its
    latitude, and read time zones from longitude. The grid
    is a geographic tool, not just a navigation tool.

  Topic Summary:
    Introduces the geographic coordinate system in full:
    lines of latitude (parallels), lines of longitude
    (meridians), the equator, the prime meridian, the
    hemisphere system, and how to read and write coordinates
    in degrees. Students practice locating places by
    coordinate pair and estimating coordinates from a map.
    The module also introduces the direct relationship
    between latitude and climate zone — which connects
    immediately to Chapter 2 (Climate Zones).

  Key Concepts:
    - Lines of latitude: parallels, equator, 0° to 90° N/S
    - Lines of longitude: meridians, prime meridian, 0° to
      180° E/W
    - How to read a coordinate pair (degrees, minutes optional
      at 9th grade introduction level)
    - Hemispheres: N/S and E/W
    - Latitude as a climate indicator — why temperature
      generally decreases with distance from the equator
    - Locating cities and geographic features by coordinate

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Understand → Apply
  Prerequisites:          Module 1.0; Module 1.1 (scale —
                          coordinates are meaningless without
                          understanding what they're measuring
                          relative to)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2, Std 3

  Sequencing Note:
    Placed after scale because coordinate reading requires
    students to already understand that maps represent real
    distances. The latitude-climate connection is introduced
    here but not developed — that belongs to Chapter 2.
    This module plants the flag; Chapter 2 builds on it.
    The POC practice questions for latitude/longitude live
    here and are promoted to full module status.

Module 1.3 — Map Projections: Every Map Lies
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-004
  Weight:           Heavy
  Estimated Time:   4–5 sessions
  Content Status:   Partial — POC includes interactive
                    projection explorer and 2 practice
                    questions. Full module spec not yet built.

  Enduring Understanding:
    It is mathematically impossible to represent a sphere on
    a flat surface without distortion. Every map projection
    distorts something — shape, area, distance, or direction.
    The question is never "which map is accurate?" It is
    "which distortion serves this purpose?" A student who
    understands this can select the right projection for
    a task and read any projection critically.

  Topic Summary:
    Introduces the projection problem — why flattening a
    sphere requires distortion — and covers the major
    projection families: cylindrical (Mercator), conic,
    and azimuthal. Students learn what each projection
    preserves and what it distorts, and practice selecting
    the appropriate projection for a described geographic
    task. The POC interactive projection explorer lives
    here and is promoted to full module status.

  Key Concepts:
    - Why projections are necessary and why distortion
      is unavoidable
    - Cylindrical projections (Mercator) — preserves shape
      and direction, distorts area at high latitudes
    - Equal-area projections (Peters/Gall-Peters) — preserves
      relative area, distorts shape
    - Conic projections — best for mid-latitude regions
    - The Mercator distortion problem: why Greenland appears
      the same size as Africa on a Mercator map `[VERIFY]`
    - Selecting projections by purpose: navigation vs.
      area comparison vs. regional display

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Understand → Apply
  Prerequisites:          Module 1.0; Module 1.2 (latitude and
                          longitude — projections are a way of
                          representing the coordinate grid on a
                          flat surface)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2

  Sequencing Note:
    Placed after the coordinate system because projections
    are best understood as different ways of representing
    the same coordinate grid on a flat surface. Students
    who understand latitude and longitude can see what a
    projection is doing to those lines — stretching,
    compressing, curving them — which makes the concept
    concrete rather than abstract.

Module 1.4 — Legends, Symbols & Map Types
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-005
  Weight:           Moderate
  Estimated Time:   3–4 sessions
  Content Status:   Partial — POC includes 1 practice question
                    on legend/symbols. Full module spec not
                    yet built.

  Enduring Understanding:
    A map without a legend is a picture without a caption —
    you can see it but you cannot read it. Map symbols and
    legends are a language, and like any language, they have
    conventions and variations. A student who can read any
    map's legend can extract information from maps they have
    never seen before. That transferable reading skill is
    the goal.

  Topic Summary:
    Covers the full range of map symbols, legend structures,
    and the relationship between symbol type and map purpose.
    Students learn to read physical maps (elevation,
    landforms), political maps (borders, capitals, cities),
    and thematic maps (population density, climate zones,
    economic data). The module also introduces how color,
    line weight, and symbol shape encode different types
    of geographic information.

  Key Concepts:
    - Legend structure: what a complete legend contains
    - Symbol types: point, line, and area symbols
    - Color conventions in geography maps (blue = water,
      green = lowland, brown = elevation — and why these
      conventions exist)
    - Thematic map types: choropleth, dot density,
      proportional symbol, isoline
    - Reading a map you've never seen: the transferable
      skill of legend literacy
    - Why the same place looks different on a physical vs.
      political vs. thematic map

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Remember → Apply
  Prerequisites:          Module 1.0; Module 1.3
                          (projections — students should
                          understand that map type and
                          projection are separate choices)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2

  Sequencing Note:
    Placed after projections because students can now see
    a map as a designed artifact with two independent sets
    of decisions: the projection (how the sphere is
    flattened) and the symbology (what information is
    encoded on that flattened surface). Moderate weight
    because the concepts are largely observational —
    the application load is lower than scale or coordinates.

Module 1.5 — Cardinal & Intermediate Directions: Orientation on the Map
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-006
  Weight:           Light
  Estimated Time:   2–3 sessions
  Content Status:   Partial — POC includes 1 practice question
                    on cardinal directions. Full module spec
                    not yet built.

  Enduring Understanding:
    Direction on a map is not intuitive — "up" is a
    convention, not a geographic fact. North is not
    up on every map ever made, and understanding why
    north became the convention, and how to orient
    yourself on any map regardless of convention,
    is a geographic literacy skill. Once learned,
    it is automatic — but it must be explicitly taught.

  Topic Summary:
    Covers the eight-point compass rose (cardinal and
    intermediate directions), how to orient a map to
    the real world, and how to use direction to describe
    relative location. Students practice describing
    geographic relationships using direction ("France
    is northeast of Spain"; "the river flows southwest
    before reaching the coast") and learn to use
    compass direction in combination with coordinates
    and scale for full location description.

  Key Concepts:
    - Cardinal directions: N, S, E, W
    - Intermediate directions: NE, NW, SE, SW
    - The compass rose on a map and what it indicates
    - Why north-up is a convention, not a geographic rule
    - Relative location using direction + distance
    - Combining direction with coordinates and scale
      for complete geographic description

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Remember → Apply
  Prerequisites:          Module 1.2 (coordinates — direction
                          is most useful when combined with
                          a coordinate reference point);
                          Module 1.4 (map reading — direction
                          is applied to reading any map)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2

  Sequencing Note:
    Placed last within the chapter by design. Cardinal
    directions are cognitively simple but functionally
    dependent on all the prior tools — a student who
    can give a complete geographic location description
    uses scale (how far), coordinates (where exactly),
    and direction (which way) together. This module
    completes that integration. Light weight because
    the concept itself is low-difficulty; the value
    is in the synthesis with prior tools.

Module 1.6 — Spatial Thinking: Putting the Tools Together
  Module ID:        GEO-MS-007
  Weight:           Moderate
  Estimated Time:   3–4 sessions
  Content Status:   Not started

  Enduring Understanding:
    The five map tools — scale, coordinates, projections,
    legends, and direction — are not separate skills. They
    are one skill: reading the world spatially. A student
    who has learned each tool in isolation needs a module
    that requires all five to work together on a real
    geographic problem. That integration is what spatial
    thinking actually is — and it is what every subsequent
    chapter in this course requires.

  Topic Summary:
    The synthesis and transfer module for Chapter 1. Students
    apply all five map tools to a single extended geographic
    problem — analyzing a real or realistic map scenario
    that requires scale calculation, coordinate reading,
    projection awareness, legend interpretation, and
    directional description to answer a geographic question.
    No new tools are introduced. The cognitive demand comes
    entirely from integration.

    This module also introduces the geographic question
    habit explicitly: after any map, ask "where?", "how
    far?", "why there?", and "what does this map hide?"
    Students practice this habit on one final map before
    Chapter 2.

  Key Concepts:
    - The spatial thinking habit: where, how far, why there,
      what's hidden
    - Applying all five map tools to a single problem
    - Map selection: given a geographic task, choosing the
      right map type, projection, and scale
    - Spatial reasoning as the bridge from Chapter 1 to
      every regional module that follows
    - Transfer task: using map tools on a map type or region
      not previously seen

  Bloom Entry / Target:   Apply → Analyze
  Prerequisites:          Modules 1.0–1.5 (all map tools)
  NCGE Standards:         Std 1, Std 2, Std 3

  Sequencing Note:
    Final module of Chapter 1 by design. Students cannot
    synthesize tools they have not yet learned — this module
    only works at the end. The Analyze-level ceiling is
    intentional: students who leave Chapter 1 at Apply
    will struggle with Chapter 2's climate zone analysis.
    Module 1.6 pushes them to Analyze before the regional
    work begins. It is also the handoff module to the spiral
    — every Chapter 2 and beyond practice set will pull
    from Modules 1.1–1.5 as spiral questions. Module 1.6
    establishes that those tools are expected to be active,
    not inert.

CHAPTER 1 ARCHITECTURE SUMMARY

Total Modules:      7 (1.0–1.6)
Required for pilot: All 7 — Chapter 1 is the prerequisite
                    to everything. No pilot works without it.

Phase Mapping within Chapter:
  Orientation:     1.0 (conceptual frame — no tools yet)
  Tool acquisition: 1.1–1.5 (five discrete tools, linear
                    dependency chain)
  Synthesis:       1.6 (all tools together — transfer task)

Bloom Arc across Chapter:
  1.0 → Remember/Understand   (what maps are)
  1.1 → Understand/Apply      (scale calculation)
  1.2 → Understand/Apply      (coordinate reading)
  1.3 → Understand/Apply      (projection selection)
  1.4 → Remember/Apply        (legend literacy)
  1.5 → Remember/Apply        (directional description)
  1.6 → Apply/Analyze         (spatial thinking synthesis)

Weight Distribution:
  1.0 is the shortest module — orientation only.
  1.1, 1.2, 1.3 are the heaviest — these are the tools
  with the highest procedural load and the most common
  misconceptions. 1.4 and 1.5 are lighter because the
  concepts are more observational. 1.6 is moderate —
  no new content, but the integration demand is real.

Build Priority:
  → 1.0 Not started                [build first]
  → 1.1 Partial (POC — scale)      [promote to full spec]
  → 1.2 Partial (POC — lat/lng)    [promote to full spec]

  → 1.5 Partial (POC — directions) [promote to full spec]
  → 1.6 Not started                [build last]
