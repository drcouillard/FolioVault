## Q1

**Bloom:** Apply · **Difficulty:** Foundational · **Tag:** [NEW: Brazil — Amazon Physical Geography]

The Amazon River carries approximately 20% of all freshwater that drains into Earth's
oceans — more than the next seven largest rivers combined. [VERIFY] A student is asked
to identify the primary geographic reason for this extraordinary volume. Which answer
is best supported by geographic evidence?

- A) The Amazon is the world's longest river, giving it the most distance to collect
  tributary water.
- **B — CORRECT) The Amazon basin sits along the equator, where the Intertropical Convergence Zone brings consistent rainfall year-round with no extended dry season.**
- C) The Andes Mountains block all moisture from reaching the Pacific Ocean and redirect
  it into the Amazon basin.
- D) The Amazon drains the world's largest watershed — approximately 7 million square
  kilometers — and a larger drainage basin area always produces a higher river
  discharge volume.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

The Amazon is not the world's longest river — the Nile holds that title by traditional
measurement (~6,650 km vs. the Amazon's ~6,400 km [VERIFY]). More importantly, length
and discharge volume are different geographic qualities. A river's volume depends on
how much precipitation falls across its drainage basin and the size of that basin —
not on how far the water travels. Notice the distinction: *how far* water moves vs.
*how much* water falls into the system. Those are separate geographic questions. To
find what drives the Amazon's volume, follow the water to its source — not the river's
length, but the precipitation falling into the basin from above.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

The Andes do play a role — they form the western wall of the Amazon basin and force
moisture-laden air upward along their eastern slopes, increasing precipitation there.
But they don't block *all* moisture from the Pacific; they intercept some of it. More
importantly, the Andes are one contributing factor, not the primary driver of the
Amazon's volume. The core mechanism is equatorial position: the ITCZ brings
year-round convective rainfall to the basin with no dry season interruption. That
consistent rainfall input — not a single geographic barrier — is what makes the
Amazon's discharge extraordinary.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

The Amazon does drain the world's largest watershed — approximately 7 million km²
[VERIFY]. A large drainage basin can collect precipitation across a wider area, which
matters. But basin size alone doesn't guarantee high discharge volume. The Nile drains
a vast basin stretching across northeastern Africa, yet its discharge is a small
fraction of the Amazon's — because much of the Nile's basin is desert, receiving
minimal rainfall. The determining factor isn't how large the basin is; it's how much
precipitation falls across it. Equatorial position drives that rainfall through the
ITCZ; basin size determines how much land is available to collect it. Both contribute
— but if the Amazon basin sat in an arid zone, its size alone would not produce
extraordinary discharge. Follow the water to its source: precipitation input, not
catchment area, is the primary geographic driver.

## Q2

**Bloom:** Apply · **Difficulty:** Foundational · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Climate & Weather Systems — Tropical Climate Zone Classification]

A geographer is describing the climate of Manaus, a city located near the equator in
Brazil's Amazon basin. Which climate classification most accurately describes Manaus,
and what is the key characteristic that distinguishes it from Brazil's other major
climate zones?

- A) Semi-arid (BSh) — long dry seasons and sparse vegetation adapted to persistent
  moisture deficit
- B) Humid subtropical (Cfa) — mild winters and hot, humid summers with year-round
  precipitation
- **C — CORRECT) Tropical rainforest (Af) — consistently high temperatures and rainfall distributed throughout all twelve months with no significant dry season**
- D) Tropical savanna (Aw) — high temperatures year-round with a pronounced dry season
  lasting several months

**Correct answer: C**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Semi-arid (BSh) describes Brazil's northeastern *sertão* — the interior of Ceará,
Piauí, and Pernambuco — where precipitation falls below evapotranspiration rates for
most of the year. Manaus sits near 3°S latitude in the Amazon basin, where the ITCZ
delivers rainfall consistently across all twelve months. The geographic principle:
latitude and position relative to the ITCZ determine moisture availability. Equatorial
regions receive the sun nearly directly overhead year-round, which drives continuous
evaporation and convective rainfall. That's the opposite of semi-arid conditions.

### Feedback — Option B (wrong)

Humid subtropical (Cfa) describes southern Brazil — Curitiba, Porto Alegre, and the
states of Paraná, Santa Catarina, and Rio Grande do Sul. The defining marker is a
mild winter (the "C" prefix in Köppen classification), which reflects those states'
distance from the equator. Manaus is near 3°S — far too close to the equator for
meaningful seasonal temperature variation. Notice the pattern: both Cfa and Af can
be described as "humid," but the similarity ends there. One has seasons; the other
doesn't. Latitude determines which applies.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Tropical savanna (Aw) classifies the *cerrado* — Brazil's vast interior plateau
covering much of Mato Grosso, Goiás, and Minas Gerais. The distinguishing feature is
a pronounced dry season (the "w" in Köppen stands for *Winterdürre* — dry winter).
Both Af and Aw are tropical, but Aw has a dry season and Af does not. Manaus and the
Amazon basin receive rainfall every month — there is no dry season to speak of. That
single distinction — dry season present or absent — separates the cerrado's climate
from the Amazon's and shapes everything from vegetation type to agricultural potential.

## Q3

**Bloom:** Apply · **Difficulty:** Standard · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Landforms & Physical Geography — Continental Drainage Divides]

The Amazon basin drains northeastward into the Atlantic Ocean. The Paraná River drains
southward, eventually reaching the Río de la Plata estuary shared by Argentina and
Uruguay. A student notices that these two major river systems drain in opposite
directions from within the same country. What geographic feature best explains why
the Amazon and Paraná basins drain in such different directions?

- A) The equator divides Brazil into two drainage regions — rivers north of the equator
  flow east, while rivers south of it flow south toward the Río de la Plata.
- **B — CORRECT) The Brazilian Highlands act as a continental divide — elevated terrain in the interior plateau separates northward and westward drainage toward the Amazon from southward drainage toward the Paraná basin.**
- C) The Andes Mountains on Brazil's western border force rivers to flow either north
  or south along the base of the range.
- D) Atlantic Ocean currents pull river drainage toward different coastal areas depending
  on latitude.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

The equator does cross northern Brazil, but latitude alone doesn't determine drainage
direction — elevation does. The mouth of the Amazon is actually slightly south of the
equator (Belém sits near 1°S), and the Paraná drains far south of the equator
regardless. Rivers follow gravity and topographic gradients, not imaginary lines.
The geographic principle to apply: find the high ground, and you find the drainage
divide. The equator is a latitude line, not a topographic feature.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

The Andes are a dominant landform in western South America, but they border Brazil
only at its far western edge — in the states of Amazonas and Acre, where Brazil meets
Peru and Bolivia. Most of Brazil's interior is defined by the Brazilian Highlands, a
vast central plateau, not the Andes. The Andes influence Andean drainage (rivers like
Peru's Marañón that feed the Amazon from the west), but Brazil's internal drainage
divide is the Brazilian Highlands. Knowing where each landform is located matters
before using it to explain drainage patterns.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Ocean currents influence coastal processes and climate, but they don't determine how
rivers in the interior drain. Rivers flow downhill from high terrain toward lower
terrain — the physics of gravity, not ocean surface circulation. The Amazon and
Paraná both reach the Atlantic; the direction each takes through the interior is set
by the topography they flow across, not by what's happening offshore. Notice the
scale mismatch: ocean currents operate at the ocean's surface; river drainage is
governed by inland elevation gradients thousands of kilometers away.

## Q4

**Bloom:** Analyze · **Difficulty:** Standard · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Human Geography Fundamentals — Primate City Formation]

São Paulo has a metro population of approximately 22 million — more than Rio de
Janeiro (~13 million), Brasília (~4.5 million), and Salvador (~4 million) combined.
[VERIFY: metro figures, current year] A geographer studying primate city formation
would look for the historical and geographic factors that explain this disproportionate
dominance. Which explanation is best supported by geographic reasoning?

- A) São Paulo became dominant because it is Brazil's capital city, concentrating
  government functions and infrastructure investment there.
- B) São Paulo's coastal location gave it natural harbor advantages that made it the
  primary destination for international trade and immigration.
- **C — CORRECT) São Paulo's position in the interior coffee-growing region connected it to the agricultural export economy, and that economic gravity attracted industry, immigration, and infrastructure investment in a self-reinforcing cycle.**
- D) São Paulo grew because Brazil's population naturally migrates toward the country's
  geographic center, and São Paulo is located closest to the interior.

**Correct answer: C**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Brasília, not São Paulo, is Brazil's capital — and Brasília was purpose-built starting
in 1956 and inaugurated in 1960 [VERIFY] specifically to draw development *away* from
the over-concentrated southeastern coast. São Paulo's economic dominance predates any capital decision by decades; it was
already the country's wealthiest city long before Brasília existed. Capital status
concentrates government functions, not necessarily economic dominance — and in Brazil's
case, the two are deliberately in different cities. Don't assume the largest city is
the capital; in Brazil, that assumption inverts the geography.

### Feedback — Option B (wrong)

São Paulo is not a coastal harbor city. It sits on a plateau approximately 70
kilometers inland from the coast, connected to the port city of Santos by a steep
escarpment called the Serra do Mar. Santos handled São Paulo's exports, but the city
itself grew on elevated inland terrain. Rio de Janeiro has the famous natural harbor.
São Paulo's rise came from coffee agriculture in the interior Paulista plateau and
the railway network that connected those farms to Santos — not from direct coastal
harbor access. Being near a port and having harbor geography are different geographic
conditions.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Population doesn't migrate toward a country's geometric center — it migrates toward
economic opportunity. São Paulo is also far from Brazil's geographic center; the
country's interior (Mato Grosso, Tocantins, Pará) is among the most sparsely
populated territory in the country. The pattern runs opposite to what this option
describes: the interior emptied, and the southeastern cities filled. Migration follows
economic gravity — jobs, wages, infrastructure — not geometric distance from a
midpoint on a map.

## Q5

**Bloom:** Analyze · **Difficulty:** Standard · **Tag:** [NEW: Brazil — Flying Rivers and Amazon Hydrology]

Researchers have identified "flying rivers" — large-scale atmospheric moisture
transport in which water vapor released by Amazon forest trees travels southward and
falls as rainfall in the cerrado and Río de la Plata basin. A student is asked what
would most likely happen to cerrado rainfall patterns if significant Amazon
deforestation continued over several decades. Which answer demonstrates the strongest
geographic reasoning?

- A) Cerrado rainfall would increase because less forest cover means higher surface
  temperatures, which increases evaporation from soil and rivers.
- B) Cerrado rainfall would remain stable because the cerrado generates its own
  independent rainfall cycle through local evaporation.
- **C — CORRECT) Cerrado rainfall would decrease because deforestation reduces the Amazon's transpiration output, weakening the flying river system that delivers atmospheric moisture to the cerrado.**
- D) Cerrado rainfall would decrease temporarily but recover once agricultural crops
  replace the forest and resume releasing moisture into the atmosphere.

**Correct answer: C**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Higher surface temperatures from reduced shade do increase local evaporation — but
this operates at a local scale, not across the thousands of kilometers the flying
river system spans. The Amazon generates moisture through *transpiration*: trees draw
water from deep groundwater reserves and release it as vapor continuously. That
process is distinct from surface evaporation and produces far greater moisture volumes.
Remove the trees, and the moisture source is gone regardless of surface temperature
changes. Scale matters here: local temperature effects and continental moisture
transport are different geographic processes operating at different levels.

### Feedback — Option B (wrong)

The cerrado has its own wet-dry seasonal cycle, but climate research — including
modeling by Brazil's national space research institute (INPE) — has shown that the
cerrado's wet season is partially supplied by moisture transported from the Amazon
basin. The two systems are coupled, not independent. "Independence" would require
that the cerrado's rainfall came entirely from local evaporation and Atlantic moisture
— the evidence doesn't support that. When a geographic system shows measurable
connections between components, treating those components as independent understates
the actual risk.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Crops do release some moisture through transpiration, but at a fraction of the rate
of tropical rainforest. Tropical forests have root systems reaching several meters
deep, drawing groundwater continuously and releasing it year-round. Annual crops
have shallow roots and grow only during the growing season. The substitution doesn't
replace the hydrological function, and "temporary" doesn't fit the decadal timescales
at which atmospheric moisture systems respond to land-use change. When evaluating
a substitution claim, ask: does the replacement do the same work, at the same scale,
at the same rate? Here, the answer on all three counts is no.

## Q6

**Bloom:** Apply · **Difficulty:** Standard · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Map Skills — Projection Selection]

A cartographer is preparing a map of Brazil for a 9th-grade class studying how Brazil's
geographic scale shapes its regional and global role. Brazil spans approximately
4,300 km east to west and 4,400 km north to south [VERIFY]. The cartographer must choose
between a Mercator projection and an equal-area projection (such as Mollweide).
Which choice is better supported by geographic reasoning?

- A) Mercator, because it preserves the accurate shape of Brazil's coastline — the
  most important feature for students to recognize.
- B) Mercator, because it is the most commonly used projection and students will find
  it easier to interpret.
- **C — CORRECT) An equal-area projection, because it accurately represents Brazil's size relative to other countries — which matters when the lesson's central claim is that geographic scale is one of Brazil's defining geographic characteristics.**
- D) An equal-area projection, because Mercator projections cannot display countries
  located near the equator.

**Correct answer: C**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Mercator is a *conformal* projection — it preserves local shape. But for a country
as large as Brazil, distortion increases significantly toward the northern and southern
edges of the map. More importantly, map purpose drives projection choice. If the
lesson is about Brazil's geographic scale — its size relative to the contiguous US,
to Europe, to Africa — then showing an accurately sized Brazil is more useful than
a slightly shape-refined one. Ask: what geographic question is the map answering?
"How big is Brazil relative to other places?" requires an equal-area projection, not
a conformal one.

### Feedback — Option B (wrong)

Familiarity is a pedagogical consideration, but it is not a geographic reason to
choose a projection — and using the familiar option can actively mislead students.
Mercator severely exaggerates high-latitude areas (Greenland appears larger than
Africa on a Mercator map; Africa is actually about 14 times larger). If a student's
existing mental map from Mercator already distorts South America's size, repeating
Mercator reinforces that distortion rather than correcting it. The goal of a geography
lesson isn't to confirm what students already think — it's to build accurate spatial
understanding.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Mercator projections can and do display equatorial regions — in fact, Mercator was
designed for maritime navigation near the equator, where it is most accurate and
distortion is lowest. Mercator's problems are worst at the poles, not at the equator.
Brazil spans roughly 5°N to 34°S — well within the range Mercator can represent
without severe distortion. The reason to choose an equal-area projection here is not
a technical limitation of Mercator; it's a question of purpose. Area accuracy serves
this lesson's goals; shape accuracy does not.

## Q7

**Bloom:** Analyze · **Difficulty:** Challenge · **Tag:** [NEW: Brazil — Cerrado Deforestation and Agricultural Feedback]

By the 2020s, the cerrado — Brazil's tropical savanna — had lost more than 50%
of its original vegetation cover to agricultural conversion, primarily for soy and
beef production. [VERIFY: deforestation percentage] A geographer argues that continued
cerrado conversion may undermine the long-term productivity of the farmland replacing
it. Which of the following best identifies the geographic mechanism behind this
argument?

- A) Deforested land is more exposed to wind erosion, which strips away the topsoil
  that crops need to grow.
- **B — CORRECT) The cerrado's native vegetation contributes moisture to the flying river system through transpiration; removing that vegetation reduces the regional rainfall the farms themselves depend on.**
- C) As cerrado farmland expands, regional population grows, and urban water demand
  from new cities reduces the water available for agricultural irrigation.
- D) Soy crops deplete soil nutrients faster than native cerrado vegetation, requiring
  ever-increasing fertilizer inputs that eventually make farming economically
  unviable.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Wind erosion is a real agricultural risk — particularly on sandy or loamy soils in
areas where vegetation cover is removed. And it does occur in parts of the cerrado
frontier. But this option doesn't identify the *specific* mechanism the geographer's
argument requires. The claim is about a regional hydrological feedback: vegetation
removal → reduced transpiration → weakened flying rivers → less rainfall → reduced
farm water supply. Wind erosion is a local soil process, not the atmosphere-scale
mechanism connecting land use to regional rainfall. Two different geographic problems
can both be real; the question asks which one matches the stated argument.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

Urban water demand and agricultural irrigation are genuine competing pressures on
Brazilian water systems — particularly visible in the São Paulo basin. But this option
misidentifies the causal chain the geographer is describing. The argument runs through
the atmosphere (transpiration → water vapor → flying rivers → rainfall), not through
human water allocation decisions between cities and farms. Connecting deforestation
to reduced rainfall requires tracing a physical geographic process, not a human
resource management conflict. Both problems exist; only one matches the mechanism
described.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

Cerrado soils are naturally acidic and nutrient-poor — a real agricultural challenge
that Brazilian researchers addressed through limestone application and fertilizer
inputs. Soil nutrient depletion is a documented concern on the agricultural frontier.
But again, this is a different mechanism from the one the geographer identifies.
The argument is about atmospheric moisture feedback (a physical geography process),
not soil chemistry (an agronomic process). Identifying which mechanism a specific
argument uses is an analytical skill: read what the argument claims, trace the steps
it implies, and match the answer that completes that specific chain.

## Q8

**Bloom:** Evaluate · **Difficulty:** Challenge · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Climate & Weather Systems — Semi-Arid Climate and Drought Vulnerability]

Brazil's northeastern *sertão* — a semi-arid interior region across nine states —
experiences recurring drought cycles that have driven internal migration toward São
Paulo and Rio de Janeiro for over a century. A development economist argues the
problem is poverty and inadequate water infrastructure. A physical geographer argues
the problem is structural — rooted in the region's climate and bedrock geology.
Which position is better supported by geographic evidence?

- A) The economist's position is stronger: wealthier regions with better infrastructure,
  like the southeastern coast, don't experience droughts — showing that investment,
  not climate, is the determining factor.
- **B — CORRECT) The geographer's position is stronger: the sertão's semi-arid climate (BSh), irregular rainfall driven by ENSO variability and weak ITCZ influence, and crystalline bedrock that limits groundwater storage create structural drought vulnerability that exists independently of development level.**
- C) Both positions are equally valid — there is no way to determine which factor is
  more significant without additional data.
- D) The geographer's position is stronger because all semi-arid regions experience
  the same levels of poverty and migration, proving that climate determines economic
  outcomes.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

The southeastern coast doesn't experience sertão-style droughts because it has a
different climate — humid subtropical and tropical, with more reliable rainfall and
better groundwater access — not because it's wealthier. This option reverses the
causal direction: coastal climate allowed economic concentration there historically;
infrastructure followed geography, not the other way around. You can't disprove the
geographer's claim by pointing to a region with a different climate and attributing
the difference to investment levels. That comparison doesn't hold the climate variable
constant — which is exactly what you need to do to isolate the effect of
infrastructure.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

"Both are equally valid" is sometimes the right conclusion — but it requires evidence
of genuine equivalence, not just the presence of two arguments. The geographer's
position can be tested against physical data: rainfall records showing ENSO
correlation, hydrogeological surveys documenting crystalline bedrock's low permeability,
and INPE climate models. That body of evidence is substantial. Retreating to "no way
to determine" concedes more uncertainty than the evidence warrants. Evaluating
competing claims means committing to the position the evidence better supports —
not splitting the difference to avoid taking a stand.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

The claim that all semi-arid climates produce identical poverty and migration outcomes
is demonstrably false. The Atacama (Chile), the Negev (Israel), and Australia's arid
interior are semi-arid and have varying levels of economic development and population
stability. Climate creates *conditions*; it doesn't produce uniform outcomes. The
geographer's actual argument is more precise: the *sertão's specific combination* of
ENSO-linked rainfall variability, crystalline bedrock, and high evapotranspiration
creates structural vulnerability in this region. Generalizing that into "climate
determines outcomes everywhere" turns careful geographic reasoning into geographic
determinism — a different, weaker, and less defensible claim.

## Q9

**Bloom:** Analyze · **Difficulty:** Challenge · **Tag:** [SPIRAL: Human Geography Fundamentals — Push-Pull Migration Factors]

Between 1950 and 2000, Brazil's urban population grew from approximately 36% to 81% —
one of the most rapid urbanization rates of the 20th century. [VERIFY: percentages]
The primary driver was internal migration from the northeastern sertão and rural
interior toward São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro. A geographer identifies both *push*
factors (conditions driving people away from origin regions) and *pull* factors
(conditions attracting people to destination regions). Which of the following most
accurately identifies the geographic push and pull factors in Brazil's internal
migration?

- A) Push: government relocation programs that displaced rural populations.
  Pull: coastal cities offered ocean access for fishing communities.
- **B — CORRECT) Push: recurring drought and limited agricultural opportunity in the semi-arid northeast and rural interior. Pull: industrial employment, infrastructure, and services concentrated in the southeastern city corridor.**
- C) Push: Amazon deforestation forced communities out of the forest interior.
  Pull: Brasília's construction as a new capital attracted workers from across the
  country.
- D) Push: urbanization in the southeast raised land prices, pushing rural migrants
  outward. Pull: the northeast offered cheaper land for farming.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Brazil did not conduct large-scale government relocation programs driving this
migration wave — the movement was primarily voluntary and economically motivated.
And coastal fishing access wasn't a driver of migration to São Paulo, which is an
inland plateau city 70 kilometers from the coast. The pull factor for São Paulo was
industrial employment — automobile manufacturing, textiles, chemicals, and finance —
not ocean access. When identifying geographic factors, connect them to what the
evidence shows actually drove the movement, not to physical features that happen
to exist in the region.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

Amazon deforestation has displaced some forest communities — a real and ongoing
process in the Amazon frontier. But this was not the primary driver of Brazil's
mid-20th century urbanization wave, which ran from the northeastern sertão toward
São Paulo. Those are different regions and different migration corridors. Brasília's
construction (1956–1960) did attract construction workers, but most returned to
origin regions or moved elsewhere after the project ended — Brasília didn't become
a sustained pull destination for the broader migration. Real geographic analysis
requires matching specific causes to specific migration flows, not generalizing
from any available example.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

This option reverses the actual direction of the geographic relationship. The
southeast (São Paulo) was already the dominant economic center, which is why land
prices were high there — high prices are a consequence of concentration, not a cause
of rural out-migration. The northeast was economically marginal not because it was
a desirable farming destination but because of drought cycles, land tenure
concentration, and infrastructure gaps. People migrated *away* from the northeast's
marginal land conditions, not toward cheaper land. Geographic reasoning requires
tracing causation in the right direction — map the sequence of cause and effect
before committing to an answer.

## Q10

**Bloom:** Evaluate · **Difficulty:** Transfer · **Tag:** [NEW: Brazil — Amazon Governance and Global Interdependencies]

A coalition of nine South American governments has proposed a binding multilateral
treaty on Amazon forest protection, arguing that deforestation in any member country
affects rainfall patterns, biodiversity, and carbon storage across the entire basin.
Brazil — which contains approximately 60% of the Amazon biome [VERIFY] — has historically
resisted binding international agreements on its internal land use, citing national
sovereignty over its territory. Evaluate which position is better supported by the
geographic evidence about how the Amazon functions as a system.

- A) National sovereignty is better supported: since Brazil contains 60% of the
  Amazon, Brazilian policy alone has sufficient influence to protect the basin's
  overall health.
- **B — CORRECT) The multilateral treaty position is better supported: the Amazon basin spans nine countries and functions as an integrated hydrological and ecological system — deforestation anywhere in the basin affects rainfall, biodiversity, and carbon storage across national borders, making single-country governance structurally insufficient.**
- C) National sovereignty is better supported: international treaties have historically
  been ineffective at preventing deforestation, so a treaty would not change outcomes
  even if the multilateral geographic argument were sound.
- D) The multilateral treaty position is better supported because all environmental
  problems require international cooperation, and national sovereignty claims should
  not override shared natural resources.

**Correct answer: B**

---

### Feedback — Option A (wrong)

Brazil's 60% share of the Amazon does give it outsized influence — but "sufficient
influence" doesn't follow from controlling the largest portion. The flying river
system carries moisture generated in the Peruvian and Bolivian Amazon into Brazilian
agricultural zones. Deforestation in Colombia's Amazon affects forest connectivity
for species whose ranges extend into Brazil. The basin functions as an integrated
system regardless of political borders. Controlling the largest portion of a system
is not the same as controlling the system — geographic interdependence doesn't stop
at the border where your territory ends.

### Feedback — Option C (wrong)

Treaty effectiveness is a relevant policy question — but it is not geographic evidence
about how the Amazon functions as a physical system. The question asks you to evaluate
which position is supported by evidence about the Amazon's geography. A geographically
sound argument can be identified independently of whether a policy instrument would
succeed politically or historically. Separating "what the geography supports" from
"what policy might succeed" is an analytical move worth practicing: the first question
is about how the world works; the second is about how humans respond to it. Answering
the second question when asked the first produces a weaker evaluation.

### Feedback — Option D (wrong)

"All environmental problems require international cooperation" is a generalization
that doesn't do geographic work. Some environmental problems are genuinely local —
a contaminated aquifer serving one municipality doesn't require a multilateral
treaty. The Amazon case is specific: it requires multilateral governance because the
hydrological and ecological system crosses borders and functions as a unit. The
argument's strength comes from that specific mechanism — flying rivers, species
ranges, drainage basin connectivity — not from a universal rule about environmental
governance. Strong geographic evaluation names the mechanism, not the principle.
