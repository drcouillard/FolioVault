## PART B — Instructional Design Foundation

### Enduring Understanding
*(Pillar 1 — written before any content)*

Brazil's geographic scale — spanning tropical, semi-arid, savanna, and subtropical
zones across 8.5 million square kilometers — creates a country of structural
contradictions: the world's largest river system alongside some of its most
water-stressed megacities, the planet's most biodiverse ecosystem alongside its
most productive agricultural frontier. Understanding Brazil means understanding how
the same geography generates both abundance and vulnerability simultaneously — and
why both are permanent features of the country, not temporary conditions.

### Acceptable Evidence — What mastery looks like

A student can explain how Brazil's physical geography (Amazon basin, cerrado, river
systems, coastal topography, and climate zone distribution) has shaped at least two
of the following: agricultural export dominance, urbanization patterns and primate
city formation, environmental governance challenges, or its relationships with
neighboring states — using specific geographic evidence, not generalization.

### Content Type Classification *(Smith & Ragan)*

**Dominant type: Conceptual-Principle** — knowing why relationships and systems work
as they do. Students must understand the mechanisms connecting geography to outcomes
(flying rivers → rainfall → agriculture → deforestation feedback), not just name
features or describe facts.

Secondary type: Declarative — key facts, boundaries, data required to support
analysis (locations, rankings, biome percentages).

*(ARCS: Attention · Gagné Event 1)*

Brazil contains more fresh water than any country on Earth — roughly 12% of the
planet's surface freshwater flows through its rivers. [VERIFY: freshwater percentage,

### Section 5 — Cultural Geography

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Language Geography | Portuguese unified a country larger than the continental US — a remarkable fact given Brazil's size and regional diversity. The language's presence reflects the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas, which placed eastern South America in the Portuguese colonial sphere. Regional dialects (carioca in Rio, paulistano in São Paulo, nordestino in the northeast) vary significantly in vocabulary and rhythm. Indigenous language communities in the Amazon retain ~150+ living languages [VERIFY], though most are critically endangered. |
| Religious Geography | The "Evangelical wave" is one of Brazil's most geographically significant social shifts. Evangelical congregations have expanded fastest in urban peripheries (where new migrants arrive from the interior) and in the northeastern sertão (historically underdeveloped, historically Catholic). Afro-Brazilian religious traditions (Candomblé, Umbanda) are concentrated in Bahia and Rio de Janeiro — regions with the highest concentration of descendants of enslaved Africans. |
| Cultural Geographic Feature | **The Legal Amazon (Amazônia Legal):** Brazil has a federal geographic designation covering approximately 5 million km² — encompassing the Amazon basin and adjacent zones. Deforestation rates within this boundary are tracked by INPE (Brazil's space research institute) and reported internationally. The Legal Amazon boundary is a political-geographic construct that determines which land-use decisions trigger federal oversight, international scrutiny, and carbon accounting. It is one of the few cases where a geographic boundary is also a governance boundary with direct global consequences. |
| Land Use & Cultural Identity | The *fazenda* (large agricultural estate) is Brazil's dominant rural land tenure form, with roots in Portuguese colonial land grants. The MST (Movimento dos Trabalhadores Rurais Sem Terra — Landless Workers' Movement) is the world's largest landless peasant movement, engaged in ongoing land occupation protests. [VERIFY — political sensitivity ⚠️] The tension between large-scale commercial agriculture (cerrado soy) and smallholder land rights is a live geographic conflict with deep historical roots. |

### Section 6 — Connections & Relationships

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Key Regional Relationships | Brazil is the undisputed regional hegemon: its economy is larger than all other South American economies combined [VERIFY], and it shares a land border with 10 of 12 South American nations. Mercosur (Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay) is the anchor South American trade bloc, with the Brazil-Argentina relationship functioning as the region's Franco-German axis — historic rivals, now institutional partners. Amazon basin governance requires coordination with 8 other nations; Brazilian deforestation policy is therefore a regional, not just national, concern. |
| Historical Geography — Formative Event | The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas divided the undiscovered world between Spain and Portugal along a meridian west of the Cape Verde Islands. This line placed what is now Brazil in the Portuguese sphere — explaining why Brazil speaks Portuguese in a Spanish-speaking continent. Portuguese colonial expansion then pressed westward far past the original line through the *bandeirantes* (interior expeditions), establishing territorial claims that ultimately produced Brazil's current shape. The country's enormous size is not a natural given; it is a product of colonial geographic ambition compounded by 19th-century border settlements. [VERIFY: Tordesillas meridian details] |
| Global Interdependencies | Brazil holds roughly 12–13% of the world's surface freshwater [VERIFY]. It is the world's largest agricultural exporter by several measures — its land-use decisions directly affect global commodity prices and food security. The Amazon generates "flying rivers" — atmospheric moisture transport systems that deliver rainfall to the cerrado agricultural zone, the Río de la Plata basin (Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay), and parts of Bolivia — meaning deforestation in Brazil affects agricultural output and water availability in neighboring countries. Brazil is a founding BRICS member, placing it in an influential tier of emerging economies with Russia, India, China, and South Africa. The Lusophone world (Portuguese-speaking nations via CPLP) extends Brazil's cultural reach to Portugal, Angola, Mozambique, and others. |

## PART D — Voice & Presentation

### Big Idea Callback — Passive Instance
*(Mid-module — Folio answers)*

**What's the big idea here?** Brazil doesn't just contain the Amazon — the Amazon
contains Brazil. The river system drains a basin larger than the contiguous United
States. The water vapor it generates travels through the atmosphere and falls as
rain on agricultural zones a thousand kilometers to the south. Remove enough of the
forest, and you don't just lose biodiversity. You change the rainfall that grows
the soybeans that generate the export revenue that funds the country. The geography
isn't a backdrop. It's the operating system.

*Decision rule applied:* Concept has no clean analogy without oversimplifying → worked
example used (cerrado transformation, see Part E).

### Big Idea Callback — Active EIB Prompt
*(End of module — student answers)*

**What's the big idea here?** A Brazilian state government is considering approving a
large agricultural expansion zone at the southern edge of the Amazon. Supporters argue
it will generate soybean export revenue and regional employment. Critics argue it will
reduce rainfall in the cerrado agricultural zone 1,000 kilometers to the south through
disruption of the flying river system.

Walk through it:
1. What geographic mechanism connects forest removal in the Amazon to rainfall
   reduction in the cerrado? Name the process specifically.
2. Identify two stakeholders with opposing positions. How does each one's geographic
   situation — where they live, what they depend on — shape their view?
3. If you were a geographer advising the state government, what evidence would you
   require before approving or rejecting the expansion?

There's no single right answer. The reasoning is the evidence.

## PART E — Instructional Metadata

### Worked Example — Gagné Event 5: Guidance
*(The Cerrado Transformation — a geographic land use case)*

The *cerrado* is a tropical savanna covering roughly 24% of Brazil — an area larger
than Western Europe. In 1970, it was considered agriculturally useless: naturally
acidic soil, six-month dry seasons, no navigable rivers for export transport.

Brazilian agricultural researchers at EMBRAPA (the state agricultural research
agency) developed techniques to neutralize soil acidity with limestone and identified
drought-tolerant soybean varieties. By the 2020s, the cerrado produces approximately
60% of Brazil's soy harvest and is the dominant biome of the country's agricultural
frontier. [VERIFY: percentage, current year]

Notice the geographic sequence:
1. Physical geography (acidic soil + seasonal drought) → agricultural barrier
2. Technological intervention (soil correction + adapted varieties) → barrier removed
3. Agricultural expansion → cerrado deforestation (>50% of original cerrado lost) [VERIFY]
4. Cerrado deforestation → reduced local water vapor contribution to flying rivers
5. Reduced flying rivers → less rainfall in the cerrado itself → yield risk in the
   exact region being farmed

The geographic irony: the agriculture replacing the cerrado depends on the rainfall
the cerrado helps generate. This is the concept students apply in Analyze-level
questions about self-limiting geographic systems.

### Misconception Inventory

| # | Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Brazil is mostly Amazon rainforest | ~60% is Amazon biome — substantial, but the cerrado covers ~24%, the caatinga ~11%, and vast swaths of the Atlantic Forest (Mata Atlântica, once covering 15% of the country) are now less than 12% intact. The country is a mosaic of biomes, not a single forest. |
| 2 | Brasília is Brazil's largest city | São Paulo has a metro population of ~22 million — the largest in the western hemisphere. Brasília was purpose-built as the federal capital starting in 1956 and inaugurated in 1960, designed explicitly to draw development away from the over-concentrated coastal southeast. It did not replace São Paulo as the dominant city; it was never intended to. |
| 3 | Brazil speaks Spanish | Portuguese is the sole official language — a direct consequence of the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas, which placed the eastern portion of South America in the Portuguese colonial sphere. Brazil is the only major Portuguese-speaking nation on the continent. Students who assume Spanish based on South American geography will misidentify the colonial geography. |
| 4 | The Amazon belongs entirely to Brazil | ~60% of the Amazon biome falls within Brazilian territory. The basin spans 9 countries — Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Peru, Suriname, Trinidad and Tobago [VERIFY], and Venezuela. Amazon governance is a multinational coordination problem; "Brazil's Amazon" is a geographic simplification with real diplomatic consequences. |
| 5 | Brazil is a developing country in the conventional sense | Brazil is the 12th largest economy globally (2023), a founding BRICS member, a G20 economy, and the world leader in tropical agricultural technology. The more precise characterization: a highly developed economy with extreme geographic and social inequality in how development is distributed — a function of historical land tenure patterns, coastal vs. interior investment, and a plantation agriculture legacy, not underdevelopment in aggregate. |

### MOM Rule — Remediation Routing Framing

Students below the mastery threshold are routed with:
**"Let's go deeper on [concept area]"** — not presented as failure or falling behind.

Concept-area routing means a student who misses physical geography questions
receives physical geography remediation; a student who misses human geography
questions receives human geography remediation. The routing message names what
the student is going deeper into — not what they got wrong, and not a generic
"try again." Remediation content uses the same real-world framing (Amazon,
São Paulo, cerrado) to maintain geographic coherence across the re-teaching path.
